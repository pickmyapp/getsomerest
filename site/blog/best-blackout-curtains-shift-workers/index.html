How to choose blackout curtains

In this guide, blackout curtains means window coverings built with light-blocking layers or dense triple-weave fabric that keep daylight out of the bedroom so the room stays dark enough for daytime sleep. Most buying mistakes come from skipping the same few checks, so run through the list below before you compare prices.

  • Price: compare the real checkout price, including shipping, not just the list price.
  • Materials: look for clear details about fabrics and fill instead of vague marketing terms.
  • Trial period: a longer home trial makes it easier to return something that doesn’t suit you.
  • Warranty and returns: read the terms, including who pays for return shipping.

Shift workers face a harder version of the darkness problem: your body clock uses light as its main signal for when to be awake, so even ordinary daylight through curtains can fragment daytime sleep. The CDC found 61.8% of night-shift workers report short sleep duration, and light exposure is one of the most fixable causes. When you shop, check the construction, not just the word blackout: true blackout curtains use a bonded black liner or dense triple-weave fabric blocking 95 to 100 percent of light, while weaker room darkening curtains only dim the room.

How to hang blackout curtains for real daytime darkness

The fabric only does half the job; installation does the rest. Buy curtains 1.5 to 2 times as wide as the frame so deep folds block more light than a flat panel, and mount the rod 4 to 6 inches wider than the window on each side and several inches above it. A wraparound rod seals the sides, and a valance fixes the most common leak: light pouring over the top. Panels should reach the floor with a little extra length. Even then, a few slivers usually survive at the edges, which is why many shift workers add a sleep mask for the gaps.

Frequently asked questions

Do blackout curtains really block 100% of light?

No curtain blocks literally 100 percent of light on its own, but the best ones get close enough that your brain cannot tell the difference. True blackout curtains with a bonded liner or dense triple-weave fabric block 95 to 99 percent of incoming light. The rest almost always comes from installation gaps: light leaking around the sides, over the top of the rod, or under a too-short panel. Hang curtains wider and higher than the frame, use a wraparound rod, and add a valance over the top, and a good blackout curtain will make a sunny bedroom feel like midnight.

What color blackout curtains block the most light?

Darker colors block marginally more light, but construction matters far more than color. A white curtain with a bonded black liner blocks more light than a dark navy curtain made from a single thin layer, because the liner is what stops the light. If two curtains use the same triple-weave construction, the darker one leaks slightly less. For shift workers, the practical advice is to choose whatever color suits your room, as long as the product specifies a blackout liner or triple-weave fabric rather than just room darkening. One thing to note: very light-colored blackout curtains can show a faint glow when the sun hits them directly, even though almost no light passes through.

Can blackout curtains also reduce noise for daytime sleepers?

Yes, to a useful but limited degree. Heavy blackout curtains add mass and an air gap in front of the window, which softens higher-frequency sounds like traffic, voices, and birdsong. Comparison tests credit dense blackout curtains with cutting perceived noise by up to half, which can be the difference between waking up and sleeping through a garbage truck. They will not block deep bass or loud construction the way earplugs do. The best strategy is layered: heavy curtains for light and moderate noise, plus a white noise machine or earplugs for what gets through. Together, curtains and sound masking make daytime sleep dramatically easier than either one alone.

Are blackout curtains worth it if I only nap during the day?

They are worth it precisely because naps are short. A napper has 20 to 90 minutes to fall asleep and get restorative rest, and every minute spent fighting daylight is wasted. Darkness triggers your brain's sleep signals faster, so blackout curtains shorten the time it takes to fall asleep during the day. They also keep the room cooler by blocking solar heat, and a cool, dark room produces deeper naps. At around $30 for a quality set, they are one of the cheapest sleep upgrades that exist, and they pay for themselves in better rest within days. Even a 20-minute nap in a truly dark room can feel more restorative than an hour in a bright one.

How do I clean blackout curtains without ruining them?

Most blackout curtains are machine washable, but the blackout layer needs gentle treatment. Wash cold and gentle with mild detergent, and skip bleach and fabric softener, which break down the liner or coating over time. Tumble dry low or hang to dry, since high heat is the main enemy of blackout linings. Iron on low and only on the decorative face, never the liner. Vacuum with a brush attachment between washes. Note: some budget sets are hand-wash only, so check the care label before buying. Iron only on low heat and only on the decorative face side, never directly on the liner. With gentle care, blackout curtains keep blocking light for years.
