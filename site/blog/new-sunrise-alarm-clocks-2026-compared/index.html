How to choose a new sunrise alarm clock

A sunrise alarm clock does one job — waking you with gradually brightening light instead of a jolt — but the newest models have grown into bedside hubs with tracking, sound libraries and smart-home features. The checks below separate the genuinely new from the merely repackaged.

  • Sunrise quality: the sunrise simulation is the whole point. Look for adjustable duration (20–40 minutes is the sweet spot), multiple brightness levels and a color gradient that moves from deep red through orange to warm white. Cheap clocks jump from dark to bright in steps; good ones fade so smoothly you cannot spot the transitions.
  • Subscription: this is the hidden cost of 2026’s models. Hatch’s full sound library needs Hatch+ at $5.99 a month, though core alarms and sounds work without it. Lumie and Philips charge no subscription at all. Add two years of any subscription to the sticker price before you compare.
  • Placement and size: the new Hatch Sleep Clock must sit level with your bed at about chest height for its tracking sensor to work — a tall dresser or a low floor setup can rule it out. Measure your nightstand before you buy any clock with sleep tracking.
  • Sound and extras: decide whether you want a sound machine, FM radio, reading lamp or Bluetooth speaker built in. Extras are genuinely useful on a nightstand, but each one is something else that can break — and something else the app may paywall.
  • Trial and warranty: you cannot judge a sunrise alarm in a store. Favor brands with a real home trial — Hatch offers 30 nights, Lumie 45 days — and at least a one-year warranty. Sunrise alarms are the rare product where the trial matters more than the spec sheet.

Price and value

Prices stretch from $119.99 to $249.99, and the right choice depends on which premium you are willing to pay. The $80 gap between the Hatch Sleep Clock and the Restore 3 buys exactly one thing — contactless sleep tracking — so skip it if you already track sleep with a wearable. The Philips at $219.99 is the most expensive per feature, but its bedroom sensor is unique. The best value is arguably the Dekala Arches: at $119.99 with no subscription, it undercuts every big name while adding a Bluetooth speaker and smart-lamp features they lack. For night-shift workers whose “morning” lands at odd hours, pair any of these with our blackout curtain guide and our night-shift sleep schedule guide — a sunrise alarm only works if the room is dark enough for the light to matter.

The big story of 2026: the clock that watches you sleep

For years, sunrise clocks and sleep trackers lived on opposite sides of the nightstand. The Hatch Sleep Clock merges them: a radar-based sensor detects your breathing and movement through the night and estimates your sleep timing and stages, with Hatch claiming 97% sleep-detection accuracy against clinical polysomnography. Nothing goes on your wrist, under your mattress or on your finger — the clock just sits there and watches.

That convenience comes with real constraints. The sensor needs the clock positioned level with your bed at roughly chest height, which rules out some nightstand setups. And it is deliberately not a full health tracker: no heart rate, no blood oxygen, no temperature — the data is sleep timing, stages and patterns, plus personalized recommendations in the Hatch app. Hatch also includes sleep tracking with the device itself, with no subscription required for the core insights; the optional Hatch+ membership ($5.99 a month or $59.99 a year) unlocks the expanded library of 2,500+ sounds, stories and meditations.

Is it worth $80 more than the Restore 3? Only if you want sleep data without wearing anything. If you already own a tracker you like, the Restore 3 gives you the same sunrise, the same sunset routines and the same sound machine for less. But for the large group of people who find wearables uncomfortable and under-mattress mats fiddly, contactless tracking from the nightstand is the easiest sleep data ever offered — and that is why this is the launch that defines the category this year.

How to get the most from a sunrise alarm

A sunrise alarm works with your biology, not against it — but only if you set it up right. First, give it a clear line of sight. The light needs to reach your closed eyelids, so angle the clock toward the bed and keep it within a few feet. A clock facing the wall is an expensive nightlight.

Second, set the sunrise duration to 30 minutes and the final brightness to the maximum you can tolerate. Shorter simulations feel abrupt; dimmer ones may not register through closed eyelids, especially in summer when ambient light competes. Most people need a week to find their setting — start bright and long, then dial back.

Third, keep a consistent wake time, even on weekends. The sunrise cue trains your circadian rhythm, but it cannot do that if the target moves by two hours every Saturday. Consistency is the multiplier: the same light at the same time each day is what shifts your body clock earlier and makes mornings genuinely easier within a couple of weeks.

Finally, use the sunset side too. Every clock in this guide simulates dusk as well as dawn, dimming gradually before bed. Pair that wind-down with a real evening routine — our 30-minute evening routine is built for exactly this — and the clock becomes a full circadian system rather than a fancy alarm. And one honest caveat: if you are a truly heavy sleeper, light alone may not cut it. Our heavy-sleeper guide explains how to layer sound with light so you actually get up.

Frequently asked questions

Do sunrise alarm clocks actually work?

For most people, yes — with realistic expectations. Gradually increasing light before your alarm suppresses melatonin and eases the transition out of sleep, which studies link to feeling more alert and less groggy on waking. They work best in a dark room with a consistent wake time. They will not fix chronic sleep deprivation; nothing replaces enough hours in bed.

Is the Hatch Sleep Clock worth $80 more than the Restore 3?

Only if you want sleep tracking without wearing anything. The two clocks share the same sunrise quality, sunset routines and sound machine — the $80 premium buys the Sleep Clock’s contactless sleep tracking and personalized insights. If you already track sleep with a watch, ring or app you like, the Restore 3 is the smarter buy.

Do I need the Hatch+ subscription?

No. Both the Sleep Clock and the Restore 3 work as sunrise alarms and sound machines with their included sounds and no ongoing payment — and sleep tracking on the Sleep Clock needs no subscription either. Hatch+ ($5.99 a month or $59.99 a year) only expands the library to 2,500+ sounds, stories, meditations and audiobooks. Try the included content first; upgrade only if you run out.

Can a heavy sleeper rely on light alone?

Often not — and the brands are honest about this. Light is a gentle cue, not a fire alarm. Truly heavy sleepers do best layering the sunrise with sound: set the light to start 30 minutes early and add a wake sound at full brightness near the end. Our guide to the best sunrise alarm clock for heavy sleepers covers exactly which combinations work.

How long should the sunrise simulation last?

Thirty minutes is the sweet spot for most people — long enough to feel gradual, short enough to fit a normal routine. Start at maximum tolerable brightness; a dim simulation may not register through closed eyelids, especially in summer. Give any new setting a full week before judging it, since your body needs several mornings to link the light cue with waking.
