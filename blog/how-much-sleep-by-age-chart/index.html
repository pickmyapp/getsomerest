How much sleep do you really need by age (chart)

From 14–17 hours for newborns to 7–8 for older adults: the expert-backed sleep chart for every age, how to read it, and what to do when your nights fall short.

Share
10 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Oct 13, 2026 · Last updated Oct 13, 2026

Everyone has heard "get eight hours," but eight hours is only the right answer for some of us. A newborn and a retiree live in completely different sleep worlds, and even teenagers and adults differ by more than most people realize. Here is the chart sleep experts actually use.

Quick answer

Newborns need 14–17 hours, infants 12–15, toddlers 11–14, preschoolers 10–13, school-age children 9–11, teenagers 8–10, adults 18–64 need 7–9, and adults 65+ need 7–8 hours per night (National Sleep Foundation). The ranges matter more than any single number — and roughly one in three adults gets less than 7.

The sleep-by-age chart

The ranges below come from the National Sleep Foundation's expert panel recommendations, the most widely cited guidelines in sleep science. Find your age group, aim for the range, and read the section below the chart for what changes at each stage of life.

How to read the chart

The most important thing about this chart is that every entry is a range, not a target. Sleep need varies between individuals the way height does: most people cluster in the middle, some healthy people sit at the edges, and a few sit outside entirely. If you consistently wake up feeling rested on 6 hours 45 minutes and you are not mainlining caffeine, you probably do not need to force yourself to 8. If you sleep 8 and still feel wrecked, the problem is more likely sleep quality — fragmentation, apnea, an uncomfortable bed — than the number.

What the chart does not measure is consistency and timing. Someone sleeping midnight to 8am nightly will almost always feel better than someone averaging the same hours with a chaotic schedule. Regularity — the same bedtime and wake time — is one of the strongest predictors of feeling rested, and it costs nothing. If you take one action from this article, make it a fixed wake time.

Finally, treat the "may be appropriate" column as permission, not pressure. A teenager who thrives on 7.5 hours is fine. An adult who genuinely needs 9.5 is fine too. The chart is a starting point for a conversation with yourself — or with a healthcare professional if something feels off — not a report card. With that framing, here is what changes at each stage of life, and what each stage most often gets wrong.

Babies and children

For the under-six crowd, total hours include naps — and the numbers surprise most new parents. Newborns need 14 to 17 hours, but they take it in short stretches around the clock, which is why the first months feel like controlled chaos. Infant sleep cycles run about 50 minutes versus an adult's 90, so babies surface between cycles and often need help settling back down. This is developmentally normal, not a problem to fix, and night waking typically consolidates over the first year. If you are worried about your baby's sleep patterns or your own exhaustion is becoming unmanageable, talk to your pediatrician or healthcare provider.

For toddlers and preschoolers the lever is the routine, not the clock: the same sequence — bath, books, lights out — at the same time nightly becomes a sleep cue. The common mistake is the drifting bedtime: 8pm becomes 9, and mornings become a war zone. A consistent wake time anchors the day, including naps — if afternoon naps push bedtime past 9pm, the nap is too long or too late. For school-age children, the enemy is the screen in the bedroom.

Teenagers

Teenagers are the most sleep-deprived group in the chart relative to their needs. They need 8 to 10 hours, but biology works against them: during puberty, the evening release of melatonin shifts later, so a teenager's body genuinely is not ready for sleep at 10pm. Combine that with early school start times, homework, and phones, and the average teen gets far less than they need — then tries to repay the debt with 12-hour weekend lie-ins that wreck the body clock further.

Weekend catch-up helps with immediate grogginess but does not fully repay a week of short nights, and the shifting schedule worsens Monday mornings by misaligning the body clock. The realistic fix is protecting the morning: consistent wake time, bright light soon after waking, and screens genuinely off 30 to 60 minutes before bed. For teens who struggle to wake, a sunrise alarm clock eases the transition with dawn simulation — one of the few gadgets with a real mechanism behind it.

Adults

Adults 18 to 64 need 7 to 9 hours, and this is where the gap between the chart and reality is widest. Roughly one in three U.S. adults regularly sleeps less than seven hours, per the CDC. The usual suspects are work schedules, caregiving, and the great modern thief: the phone in bed. What makes adult short sleep insidious is adaptation — after a few weeks of six-hour nights, you stop feeling tired but your performance stays impaired. People routinely rate themselves as fine while their reaction times say otherwise.

The adult playbook: fix the wake time first, including weekends — sleeping in more than an hour past it creates the Monday crash. Cut caffeine after midday (a 3pm coffee is still half-present at 9pm). Make the bedroom dark, cool and quiet, since fragmented sleep steals deep stages even when total hours look fine. If your schedule cannot deliver seven hours, protect what you get and nap strategically: our power nap guide covers the 20-minute sweet spot, and night shift workers get a dedicated schedule.

Older adults

Here is the surprise in the chart: adults 65 and older still need 7 to 8 hours — only slightly less than younger adults. The myth that older people need much less sleep causes real harm, because it normalizes the 5-hour nights that leave people groggy and then gets blamed on age. What actually changes with age is sleep architecture: deep slow-wave sleep shrinks, sleep becomes lighter and more fragmented, and the circadian clock shifts earlier, so waking at 5am feeling done is common even after a reasonable bedtime.

Work with the new architecture: an earlier bedtime matching the shifted clock beats lying awake holding a 30-year-old schedule. Morning light exposure anchors the rhythm, and naps should stay under 30 minutes and before mid-afternoon. Frequent waking for bathroom trips, pain, or loud snoring deserves a healthcare conversation — treatable causes are common, and "I'm just old" is not a diagnosis.

Closing the gap: practical steps

If your nights fall short, do not fix everything at once — pick one lever and run it for two weeks. The highest-yield lever is a fixed wake time, seven days a week: it sets the circadian anchor, and sleep pressure builds toward an earlier bedtime on its own. The first few days are rough; it stabilizes.

The second lever is the bedroom itself. Light is the strongest time cue your brain receives, so the room should be genuinely dark: blackout curtains or a good sleep mask, no standby LEDs, no glowing chargers. Cool beats warm — around 65°F (18°C) suits most sleepers. And noise should be steady or absent, not intermittent: a consistent evening routine plus steady background sound does more for light sleepers than any gadget. Track what you actually do, not what you think you do — a simple two-week sleep diary (bedtime, wake time, how you felt at noon) reveals patterns that memory hides. If the diary shows you are doing everything right and still sleeping far less than your range, or you snore loudly, gasp, or feel exhausted despite time in bed, that is the point to talk to a healthcare professional rather than trying harder.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours of sleep do adults really need?

Most adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night, according to the National Sleep Foundation's expert recommendations. Consistently sleeping under 7 hours is associated with worse daytime functioning, and roughly one in three U.S. adults reports doing exactly that. Some people genuinely function on slightly less or need slightly more, but the 7-to-9 range is the target for the vast majority.

Do sleep needs really decrease as you get older?

Yes, but less than people think. Teenagers need 8 to 10 hours, adults need 7 to 9, and adults over 65 still need 7 to 8 hours — the drop from young adulthood to old age is small. What changes more with age is sleep architecture: older adults tend to spend less time in deep sleep, wake more easily, and shift toward earlier bedtimes, even when total sleep time stays similar.

Can teenagers catch up on sleep on weekends?

Weekend catch-up sleep helps with immediate grogginess, but it does not fully undo a week of short nights, and the shifting schedule — late nights, late mornings — can make Monday mornings worse by misaligning the body clock. A consistent bedtime that delivers 8 to 10 hours on school nights beats a 12-hour weekend lie-in for steady daytime alertness.

Is it normal for babies to wake up several times a night?

Yes, frequent night waking is developmentally normal for babies, especially in the first six months. Newborns sleep 14 to 17 hours total but in short stretches, and their sleep cycles are much shorter than an adult's. Night waking usually consolidates gradually over the first year. If you are worried about your baby's sleep or your own exhaustion, talk to your pediatrician or healthcare provider.

What should I do if I consistently sleep less than my age group needs?

Start with the boring basics: a consistent wake time (even on weekends), a dark cool bedroom, limited caffeine after midday, and screens off well before bed. Track your sleep for two weeks to see the real pattern rather than guessing. If you are doing all of that and still sleeping far less than recommended — or you snore loudly, gasp, or feel exhausted despite time in bed — talk to a healthcare professional.

Key takeaways

  • Newborns need 14–17 hours, teens 8–10, adults 7–9, and older adults still need 7–8 — the ranges matter more than any single number.
  • A fixed wake time beats a fixed bedtime as the single highest-yield habit for closing the gap.
  • Consistency and timing matter as much as total hours; weekend catch-up sleep does not fully repay weekday debt.
  • If good habits do not close the gap — or you snore, gasp, or feel exhausted despite time in bed — talk to a healthcare professional.
