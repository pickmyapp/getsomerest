Pink Noise & Bedtime Music: 2026 Sleep Study

A 2026 trial of 66 students found bedtime music plus pink noise improved self-reported sleep. What the study found, its limits, and how to try it.

Share
9 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Nov 13, 2026 · Last updated Nov 13, 2026

Pink noise has quietly become the sound of the sleep internet: softer and deeper than white noise, it turns up in sleep apps, wind-down playlists and white noise machines as the color of sound most like steady rainfall. Now a 2026 randomized controlled trial gives it a small but real evidence base. Researchers assigned 66 college students with poor sleep to one of three groups: pure music mixed with pink noise, pure music mixed with binaural beats, or no audio at all. Each audio group listened for 30 minutes at bedtime, five nights a week, for four weeks. Both audio groups reported better sleep and lower perceived stress than the control group, while binaural beats showed the clearest stress reduction. Morning cortisol did not differ between groups. The study is small, short and questionnaire-based — but one of the few head-to-head tests of pink noise against another bedtime sound.

Quick answer

In a 2026 randomized trial of 66 students with poor sleep, 30 minutes of bedtime music mixed with pink noise, five nights a week for four weeks, improved self-reported sleep quality. Music mixed with binaural beats improved sleep by a similar amount and reduced perceived stress more. Morning cortisol levels did not differ between groups, and sleep was measured by questionnaire, not by overnight brain or body recordings.

What Pink Noise Is

Pink noise is a steady, unchanging sound in which every octave carries the same amount of energy. Because higher frequencies are quieter than lower ones, it sounds deeper, softer and less hissy than white noise — the usual comparison is steady rainfall, a distant waterfall, or wind moving through leaves.

That softer profile is why pink noise has become the default recommendation for people who find white noise too harsh. White noise spreads its energy evenly across all frequencies, which the ear hears as a bright, flat hiss, like untuned radio static. Pink noise pulls the top end down, so it is easier to ignore. Brown noise goes further in the same direction — its energy falls faster with frequency, so it sounds deeper and heavier still, closer to a low rumble.

Pink noise works at bedtime the same way other steady sounds do: it masks. A sudden noise — a car door, a dog barking — wakes you because it stands out against silence. A steady bed of sound raises the background level, so those spikes stand out less. It does not cancel noise or force sleep; it makes the room's sound more even. You have probably already heard pink noise: rain, ocean waves and rustling leaves are naturally pink-noise-like. Our guide to the best ambient sounds for deep sleep covers the natural-sound end of the same idea.

The 2026 Trial: Music, Pink Noise and Binaural Beats

The study was published on June 17, 2026, in Frontiers in Psychology, by Hengfei Hou, Jingjing Wang and Xia Yue. It is a genuine randomized controlled trial: participants were randomly assigned to groups, so differences between groups are more likely to come from the audio than from who happened to sign up.

The researchers recruited 66 college students who scored above 7 on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a standard questionnaire where a score above 5 generally marks poor sleep. The students were randomly split into three groups of 22: a pink noise plus pure music group, a binaural beats plus pure music group, and a control group that received no audio. The two audio groups listened to their assigned 30-minute recording at bedtime, five nights a week, for four consecutive weeks. Sleep quality and perceived stress were measured at baseline, week 2, week 4 and a week-6 follow-up, with morning saliva samples collected at the same points to measure cortisol.

Two design details matter for how far the results reach. First, both audio groups listened to music, so with no music-only group the trial cannot separate what the music did from what the pink noise or the binaural beats did. Second, the trial ran in the students' own dorm rooms, not a sleep lab, so nobody recorded brain waves or sleep stages overnight.

Binaural beats, for those unfamiliar, are an auditory effect rather than a sound color: two slightly different tones are played into each ear, and the brain perceives a pulsing beat at the difference between them. They are often marketed as a way to steer brainwaves toward relaxation, but that claim was not tested here — the trial used no EEG recording.

Pink vs White vs Brown Noise

If you are choosing a bedtime sound, the practical differences between the three common noise colors are small but real: they differ in how bright or deep they sound and how well they cover different kinds of background noise.

Pink noiseSoft, deep, like steady rainfall3 dB drop per octaveGeneral masking; most comfortable for long listeningCan feel too soft to cover sharp noises
White noiseBright, even hiss, like radio staticEqual energy at all frequenciesCovering a wide range of background soundsCan feel harsh or tiring at higher volumes
Brown noiseDeep rumble, like strong wind or a waterfall6 dB drop per octaveLow-frequency masking; fans of very deep soundCan feel boomy in a small room; covers less high-frequency noise

The honest summary is that no color has been proven better than the others for sleep. The 2026 trial tested pink noise mixed with music against no audio — not against other noise colors. Our brown noise vs white noise comparison reaches the same conclusion: pick the sound you can listen to for half an hour without noticing it, at a volume low enough to talk over. If white noise feels like static, pink is the natural next try; if pink feels too thin, brown or white may mask better.

One practical note applies to all three: keep the volume low. The trial played its audio at bedtime-listening levels, and hearing researchers generally advise keeping any all-night sound below conversational level. A sleep timer that fades the sound out after 30 to 60 minutes gives you the masking while you are falling asleep, without running all night.

What the Study Found — and What It Didn't

The headline result is straightforward: both audio groups improved. On the PSQI sleep-quality questionnaire and the Perceived Stress Scale, the analysis found significant time, group, and time-by-group effects (all p < 0.05): the audio groups improved more than the control group, and the gains were still visible at the week-6 follow-up.

The more interesting split is on stress. Both audio groups reported lower perceived stress than the control group, but the binaural beats group improved significantly more than the pink noise group — and on sleep quality it also showed the larger PSQI drop (1.18 points by weeks 4 and 6, against 0.41 to 0.64 for pink noise). The PSQI runs from 0 to 21 and a score above 5 marks poor sleep, so a drop of around one point is modest, not a transformation.

What the study did not find is just as important. Morning salivary cortisol — a single sample taken 30 minutes after waking — showed only a main effect of time, with no significant differences between groups. In plain terms, the students felt less stressed, but their morning cortisol did not show it. The authors note one morning sample cannot capture how the stress-hormone system behaves across the day, so the trial could not detect a hormonal change, not that none happened.

And the caveats stack up. Sixty-six people is a small trial, and 22 per group leaves little room for error. Adherence was self-reported, and follow-up ended two weeks after the intervention, so nobody knows whether the benefit lasted. None of this makes the study useless; it makes it a first step. A cheap, low-risk routine that improved self-reported sleep in a randomized trial is worth trying for a few weeks and judging by how you feel — it is not a treatment, and it is not proof that pink noise does something special to the brain.

How to Try Pink Noise at Bedtime

Trying this at home is simple, cheap and low-risk, and the trial gives you a ready-made protocol to copy: 30 minutes of audio at bedtime, five nights a week, for four weeks, then judge the result. Start with a pink noise track mixed with slow, lyric-free music if you want to match the study, or plain pink noise if you prefer it — the trial cannot tell you which half did the work, so comfort is a fair tiebreaker.

Pick your source. Most sleep apps include pink noise, and our roundup of the best white noise app for sleep covers which ones offer it. If you would rather not have a phone in the bedroom, a dedicated machine is the better buy: the LectroFan sound machine on Amazon is one of the widely sold models that includes pink noise — check the sound list before ordering, because not every machine offers it.

Set the volume low. Start at the lowest level where the sound is audible and comfortable, roughly the volume of a quiet conversation — you should still be able to hear someone speak over it. If you share a bed, try sleep headphones or a pillow speaker — see our guide to the best sleep headphones for side sleepers.

Use a timer and a routine. Set the sound to fade out after 30 to 60 minutes, matching the trial's 30-minute dose, and pair it with the rest of your wind-down: dim the lights, put the phone away, and keep the same bedtime for a few weeks. Our 30-minute evening routine and the wind-down routine for night owls both slot a sound track into a wider routine.

Judge it after four weeks. Keep a simple note each morning of how long you think it took to fall asleep and how rested you feel, then compare week one with week four. If you feel no difference after a month, pink noise probably is not your sound — try brown noise, a different music mix, or no sound at all. And a plain note: if your sleep problems are persistent, severe, or come with loud snoring, gasping, or overwhelming daytime sleepiness, that is a conversation for a doctor, not a sound machine.

Frequently asked questions

What is pink noise, and how is it different from white noise?

Pink noise is a steady sound whose power falls by 3 decibels per octave, so lower frequencies carry more energy and it sounds deeper and softer than white noise — more like steady rainfall than radio static. White noise spreads energy evenly across all frequencies and sounds brighter and harsher. Brown noise falls faster still, at 6 decibels per octave, and sounds deeper than pink noise.

Did the 2026 study prove pink noise improves sleep?

Not conclusively. In the trial, 66 students with poor sleep listened to music mixed with pink noise or with binaural beats for 30 minutes at bedtime, five nights a week for four weeks, and both audio groups reported better sleep than a no-audio control group. But sleep was measured by questionnaire only, there was no music-only group, and the sample was small and made up of students.

Was pink noise or binaural beats better in the trial?

Both improved self-reported sleep by a similar amount, but binaural beats mixed with music reduced perceived stress more than pink noise mixed with music. The binaural group's PSQI score fell by 0.86 points at week 2 and 1.18 points at weeks 4 and 6, compared with a 0.41 to 0.64 point fall in the pink noise group. Neither group differed on morning cortisol.

How long should I listen to pink noise before bed?

The trial used 30 minutes of audio at bedtime, five nights a week, for four weeks — so 30 minutes is the tested dose. Start the sound when you get into bed, keep the volume low enough that you can still hear a normal conversation over it, and use a sleep timer so it fades out after 30 to 60 minutes.

Do I need a special device to play pink noise?

No. Most sleep and white noise apps include a pink noise setting, and many white noise machines and smart speakers do too — check the sound list before you buy, since not every machine offers it. A phone on a nightstand works, though a dedicated machine or a small speaker keeps the phone out of the bed.

Key Takeaways

  • The study: a 2026 randomized trial (Hou et al., Frontiers in Psychology) assigned 66 students with poor sleep to music plus pink noise, music plus binaural beats, or no audio — 30 minutes at bedtime, five nights a week, four weeks.
  • What improved: both audio groups reported better sleep and lower perceived stress than the control group, with gains still visible at a week-6 follow-up. Binaural beats plus music reduced perceived stress more, with the larger sleep-score drop (1.18 vs 0.41–0.64 points).
  • What didn't: morning cortisol showed no group difference, and sleep was measured by questionnaire only — no overnight brain recording, no sleep staging, no music-only group.
  • What pink noise is: a steady sound with more low-frequency than high-frequency energy, softer and deeper than white noise — like steady rainfall — used to mask background noise.
  • How to try it: 30 minutes at bedtime, low volume, sleep timer on, for four weeks, then judge by how you feel.
  • The limit: a small, short, student-only trial of a low-risk routine — worth trying, not a treatment, and persistent or severe sleep problems belong with a doctor.
