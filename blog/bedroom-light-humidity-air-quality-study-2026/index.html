Bedroom Light and Air Quality: What the 2026 Study Found

In 29 older adults tracked for 211 nights, bedroom light was the strongest link to sleep time — while humidity, temperature and PM2.5 pulled different levers.

Share
8 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Nov 10, 2026 · Last updated Nov 10, 2026

Your bedroom is a sleep environment, not just a room — and a new study measured exactly how much each part of it matters. Researchers at Oregon Health & Science University tracked 29 older adults in their own Portland bedrooms for an average of 211 nights, recording light, noise, temperature, humidity and air quality alongside objective sleep data. The headline finding is that different factors pull different levers: light was the strongest link to how long people slept, humidity to how much they woke during the night, temperature to how much they tossed and turned, and fine-particle pollution to both sleep length and efficiency. Here is what the study measured, what each factor did, and what it means for the room you sleep in.

Quick answer

The short answer: In 29 older adults tracked for 211 nights, bedroom light was the strongest link to total sleep time, humidity to waking during the night, temperature to tossing and turning, and PM2.5 air pollution to both sleep length and efficiency. Small, observational, and older-adult-only — but unusually precise about which factor affects which part of sleep.

At a Glance: What the Study Found

Most bedroom advice treats the room as a single problem — make it dark, cool and quiet. A new study from Oregon Health & Science University suggests the reality is more specific: each part of the bedroom environment tracks with a different part of sleep. The team put sensors in the bedrooms of 29 older adults in Portland and recorded light, noise, temperature, humidity, fine-particle pollution, volatile organic compounds and carbon dioxide alongside objective sleep data, for an average of 211 nights per person.

  • Light → how long you sleep: light level had the strongest association with total sleep time. Brighter bedrooms tracked with shorter sleep.
  • Humidity → how much you wake: relative humidity was the strongest predictor of wake-after-sleep-onset and sleep efficiency — the largest effects in the whole study.
  • Temperature → how restless you are: temperature was most strongly tied to toss-and-turn count, and to overnight heart rate and breathing rate.
  • PM2.5 → duration and efficiency: fine-particle pollution was strongly tied to both total sleep time and sleep efficiency.
  • Small but deep: only 29 people, all older adults (average age about 77), but an average of 211 nights each — and every reported association passed a strict significance threshold.

The study was published in E3S Web of Conferences 716, 05024 (2026). It is observational, so it shows which bedroom factors track with which sleep outcomes — not proof that changing them will change your sleep.

How to Read This Study Without Overreading It

The Portland study is unusually careful about what it claims — and the first thing to notice is its size. Twenty-nine people is small, and all of them were older adults living in one city, with an average age of about 77. Twelve of them had mild cognitive impairment or dementia. That means the findings describe older adults in Portland, not sleepers in general, and the results may not transfer to a 30-year-old in a hot apartment in Phoenix.

What the study gains in depth, it trades in breadth. A mean of 211 nights per person — with some participants tracked far longer — is an enormous amount of data per sleeper, and the measurements were objective: sensors in the bedroom, not diaries or questionnaires. The team also adjusted for the obvious confounders (age, sex, cognitive status, bed-sharing and season) and applied a strict Bonferroni correction, requiring p < 0.0083 before calling an association significant. Every association reported in this article cleared that bar.

Effect sizes are small, and that is normal. The reported effect sizes run from about 0.05 to 0.12, which means each factor explains a modest slice of night-to-night variation in sleep. Nobody's sleep is decided by one bedroom factor; the point is that across hundreds of nights, the pattern is consistent enough to be real. A small effect that shows up every night for years is still worth knowing about.

Association is not causation. The study cannot show that dimming the lights, drying the air or cooling the room would improve anyone's sleep — it can only show that, in these bedrooms, those factors tracked with those sleep outcomes. People who sleep in brighter rooms may also go to bed later, live on noisier streets or have different health. The study adjusted for several of those things, but no observational study can adjust for all of them.

How this fits with the Penn State study: the Penn State study covered in our earlier article followed 372 young adults (average age 22) for 14 days and found bright, hot and noisy bedrooms hurt sleep quality, not duration. Portland's older adults show a different pattern — light hitting duration, humidity hitting waking — which is a useful reminder that bedroom advice is not one-size-fits-all: what matters most may change with age, and the two studies measure different things in different people.

What the study did not measure: it averaged each factor per night, so it cannot say whether light at 2 a.m. matters more than light at bedtime, or whether a humid evening matters more than a humid morning.

Our Verdict: The Honest Takeaway

The study's real value is its map, not any single finding. Most bedroom advice treats the room as one thing — make it dark, cool and quiet — and this study shows why that advice is blunt: light, humidity, temperature and air quality each tracked with a different part of sleep. Light hit total sleep time hardest. Humidity hit waking and efficiency. Temperature hit restlessness and heart and breathing rates. PM2.5 hit duration and efficiency. If you can only fix one thing, the study suggests starting with light — it had the strongest link to how long older adults slept, and it is also the cheapest factor to change.

The humidity finding deserves more attention than it gets. Relative humidity was the strongest predictor of wake-after-sleep-onset (ES -0.101) and sleep efficiency (ES 0.104) — larger effects than light's on total sleep time. Most sleep advice barely mentions humidity, yet in this data it was the factor most tied to staying asleep once asleep. A cheap hygrometer tells you where your bedroom sits; very dry winter air and damp summer air are both common, and both showed up here.

Keep the sample in mind. Twenty-nine older adults in Portland cannot settle what a bedroom should be for everyone, and the study's authors do not claim otherwise. But the pattern it found lines up with independent work: the Penn State study of 372 young adults found bright, hot and noisy bedrooms hurt sleep quality; the Johns Hopkins study of 69 low-income older adults found PM exposure tied to poorer efficiency and more fragmentation; and the Boston study of 50 older adults found efficiency best between 68 and 77°F. Different cities, different ages, different methods — same direction. That convergence is what makes this small study worth more than its sample size suggests.

What the Coverage Got Right — and Where It Overreached

This study has had little mainstream coverage, which is itself a finding. Unlike the AHA irregular-sleep study, which was covered by every health desk the week it came out, the Portland paper is a conference-proceedings study that has mostly circulated among building-science and sleep researchers. That means less hype — but also less scrutiny, and fewer plain-language summaries for readers to check.

The context that matters is the Tulane light study. Published in the European Heart Journal in September 2026, Tulane's analysis of 11,071 UK Biobank participants found that nighttime light above 3 lux was linked to thicker heart walls and, in a larger 73,000-person analysis, a 24% higher risk of heart attack, 33% higher risk of stroke and 29% higher risk of heart failure. The Portland study adds the sleep mechanism to that story: light is also the bedroom factor most tied to how long older adults sleep. Two different studies, two different methods, one consistent message about light at night.

What to be careful about: the Portland study measured bedroom light as one average per night, not the timing or colour of the light, and it did not test whether blocking light improves sleep. Articles that turn this into “buy blackout curtains and your sleep will improve” are going one step further than the data. Blackout curtains are a reasonable, low-cost experiment, but the study itself only shows that brighter bedrooms tracked with shorter sleep in these 29 people.

The air-quality angle is the under-reported one. PM2.5 had effect sizes on total sleep time (-0.050) and sleep efficiency (-0.058) nearly as large as light's, and the Johns Hopkins study independently found the same link in a different group of older adults. Bedroom air quality rarely appears in sleep advice, which tends to focus on light and temperature — but two 2026 studies now point the same way, and ventilation is a fix that costs nothing on a mild night.

Frequently asked questions

What did the Portland bedroom study find?

In 29 older adults (average age about 77) tracked for a mean of 211 nights in their own Portland, Oregon bedrooms, light level had the strongest association with total sleep time, relative humidity was most strongly tied to wake-after-sleep-onset and sleep efficiency, temperature was most strongly tied to tossing and turning and to heart and breathing rates, and fine-particle pollution (PM2.5) was strongly tied to total sleep time and sleep efficiency. All reported associations passed a strict Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (p < 0.0083).

How was the study done?

Researchers at Oregon Health & Science University placed sensors in the participants' own bedrooms and collected night-time environmental data alongside objective sleep metrics for a mean of 211 nights per person. Seventeen participants had normal cognition (average age 76.6) and 12 had mild cognitive impairment or dementia (average age 77.1). The team tested seven environmental factors — light, noise, temperature, humidity, PM2.5, total volatile organic compounds and carbon dioxide — against six sleep metrics, adjusting for age, sex, cognitive status, bed-sharing and season. The paper appeared in E3S Web of Conferences 716, 05024 (2026).

Does this prove a dark, cool, clean bedroom makes you sleep better?

No — the study shows associations, not proof. It is a small observational study of 29 older adults in one city, and it cannot show that changing any single factor would improve sleep. What it does show, with unusual precision, is which bedroom factors track with which parts of sleep: light with how long you sleep, humidity with how much you wake, temperature with restlessness, and fine particles with both duration and efficiency.

Why did light matter most for total sleep time?

Light had the largest effect size on total sleep time (ES -0.051), meaning brighter bedrooms were associated with shorter sleep. That fits a separate, larger body of work: a Tulane study published in the European Heart Journal in September 2026 found that nighttime light above 3 lux — about the brightness of a phone screen at low brightness on a nightstand — was linked to heart-wall thickening and a 24–33% higher risk of heart attack, stroke and heart failure. The Portland study adds the sleep side of the same picture: light is the bedroom factor most tightly tied to how long older adults sleep.

What can I actually change in my bedroom from this?

The study points to four things worth checking, in order of what it measured: light leaks (blackout curtains or a sleep mask if street or hallway light reaches the bed), humidity (very dry or very damp air tracked with more waking — a hygrometer costs a few dollars and tells you where you stand), temperature (warmer rooms tracked with more tossing and turning, and a separate Boston study of 50 older adults found sleep efficiency best between 68 and 77°F), and air quality (PM2.5 tracked with shorter, less efficient sleep — ventilation and, where needed, a HEPA purifier are the usual fixes). Treat these as comfort and environment checks, not medical treatment.
