The Short Answer: Block, Mask, Darken, Schedule

Daytime sleep in a noisy house is a noise problem before it is a sleep problem, and no single product solves it. The plan that works has four layers, and it costs about $111 on sale — plus one free agreement.

The plan in brief: block sharp sounds with reusable earplugs — the Loop Quiet 2 at $24.95; mask steady noise with a white noise machine by the bedroom door — the Dreamegg D11 Max at $17.99 on sale, or the Yogasleep Dohm Classic at $49.99 for deeper masking; darken the room until you cannot see your hand — blackout curtains plus a Manta Sleep Mask Pro at $68 on sale; and get the household onto fixed quiet hours, with headphones for videos and games and noisy chores scheduled around your sleep. Everything below follows CDC/NIOSH guidance and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2025 guideline. If daytime sleep stays difficult despite the room and the routine, talk to a clinician, not a product page.

Why Daytime Noise Is a Different Problem

Night sleep fails quietly: a partner snores, a pipe knocks, a car passes. Day sleep fails loudly and unevenly, and the pattern matters. There are three kinds of daytime noise, and each needs a different fix.

Steady noise: traffic, appliances, rain

Steady noise is the easiest to handle. It is constant and predictable, and a machine covers it well by raising the background level until the noise blends in. Traffic hum, a washing machine, a fan, rain on the roof: pick a machine with a deep, low-frequency sound and put it between you and the noise source, and the problem mostly disappears. Our best white noise machines for night shift workers guide covers the five best picks, from $17.99 to $49.99.

Intermittent noise: voices, doors, footsteps

Intermittent noise is the hard one. A voice, a door slamming, footsteps on stairs, a dog barking: these sounds spike suddenly, and a fixed-volume machine cannot cover a spike. Earplugs handle the spike better, because they lower everything reaching your ear by the same amount — the Loop Quiet 2 cuts 24 decibels, which takes the edge off a shout without silencing an alarm. The combination is the answer: earplugs for the spikes, a machine for the background.

Structure-borne noise: footsteps above, bass through walls

Some noise travels through the building, not the air: footsteps from the flat above, bass from a television, a washing machine's spin cycle shaking the floor. No earplug and no machine fully stops it, because you feel it as much as hear it. The fixes are structural and cheap: a rug on a hard floor, a draft stopper under the door, weather stripping around the door frame, and heavy curtains over the window. If the noise comes from above, a machine placed high on a shelf masks it better than one on the floor.

The Layered Plan: Block, Mask, Darken, Schedule

Think of daytime sleep as four layers, in order. Block the sharp sounds, mask the steady ones, darken the room, and schedule the household. Miss one layer and the others have to work twice as hard.

Layer one is earplugs. The Loop Quiet 2 at $24.95 is the pick: reusable silicone, four tip sizes, 24 decibels of reduction, and flat enough to sleep on your side. Layer two is masking: the Dreamegg D11 Max at $17.99 on sale, placed near the bedroom door, covers household sound for less than twenty dollars. Layer three is darkness: the Manta Sleep Mask Pro at $68 on sale (list $85) blocks what blackout curtains miss, and its C-shaped eye cups do not press on your eyes. The Plan, Step by Step

1. Earplugs: your first line, not your only line

Start with earplugs, because they are cheap and they work on the sounds a machine cannot touch. The Loop Quiet 2 at $24.95 is the best all-round pick: soft silicone, four tip sizes so you can find a seal that does not ache, a carry case, and a 24-decibel reduction that takes the edge off voices, doors and barking without making you deaf to an alarm. It sits flush, so side sleepers can press into the pillow without pain — the reason foam plugs fail for so many people. Disposable foam plugs are the budget alternative at a few dollars a pack: they block more sound, but they press, itch, and need replacing every few days. Keep them clean — wash reusable plugs after every few uses — and do not push them deep into the ear canal. If you need to hear an alarm, set a vibrating alarm on your phone or use a sunrise alarm clock, because earplugs plus a sound machine will mute a phone speaker.

2. Masking: fill the room so the house disappears

With earplugs in, add a steady sound to fill the gaps. The Dreamegg D11 Max at $17.99 on sale is the cheapest machine that does this properly: 21 non-looping sounds, a battery that lasts up to two days, and a small speaker that suits a bedroom. Place it near the bedroom door, not next to your pillow — the job is to cover the noise coming in, not to play sound into your ear. If the noise is mostly traffic or a steady hum, the Yogasleep Dohm Classic at $49.99 is the stronger pick: a real fan makes a deep, low-frequency sound that covers voices and traffic better than any digital hiss, and it never loops.

3. Darkness: the other half of day sleep

Noise gets the attention, but light is the stronger sleep signal. The CDC/NIOSH guidance for shift workers sets the standard plainly: the bedroom should be dark enough that you cannot see your hand. Blackout curtains do the heavy lifting — fit them to overlap the window frame, because light leaks at the edges. A mask covers what the curtains miss: the Manta Sleep Mask Pro at $68 on sale (list $85) uses C-shaped eye cups that seal out light without pressing on your eyelids, and the cups slide along the strap to fit your face. The original Manta Sleep Mask at $39 does the same job with less ventilation.

4. The household: the free fix that matters most

No product replaces an agreement. Sit the household down and fix your sleep hours on the calendar — the same hours every workday, visible on the fridge — and agree what quiet means during them: headphones for videos and games, no vacuuming, no DIY, laundry and dishes before or after. Give children a concrete job: headphones on, inside voices, and a small reward for keeping to it. A washing machine with a delay timer can run while you are at work, not while you are asleep. And protect the timing: go to bed as soon as you get home, because the window for easy daytime sleep closes as the day goes on. The more sleep you get before 2 p.m., the better rested you will be — and the household's quiet hours should start the moment you walk in, not an hour later.

5. Temperature and timing: the two quiet helpers

Two things that are not about noise still decide whether you fall asleep in the day. Keep the bedroom cool, around 65 to 68 degrees — a warm room fights sleep even in silence, and daytime heat builds fast behind blackout curtains. And keep the anchor: one fixed block of sleep at the same clock time every day, whatever shift you are on, plus a second flexible block to reach at least seven hours in 24. The CDC's figures show why: 44 percent of night-shift workers reported sleeping six hours or less a day, against 28.8 percent of day workers — and the anchor stops a rotating roster resetting your body clock every week.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to block noise when sleeping during the day?

Layer three things. First, earplugs: the Loop Quiet 2 at $24.95 cuts 24 decibels and sits flush enough for side sleepers. Second, masking: a white noise machine such as the Dreamegg D11 Max at $17.99 on sale raises the background level so sudden sounds blend in. Third, the household: a quiet-hours agreement, chores scheduled around your sleep, and a draft stopper under the door. No single layer silences a house; the three together do.

Should I sleep with earplugs every day after night shift?

Reusable silicone earplugs such as the Loop Quiet 2 are made for daily use: wash them, dry them, and replace the tips when they wear out. Keep them clean — dirty earplugs are the main cause of ear irritation — and do not push them deep into the canal. If you need to hear an alarm, set a vibrating alarm on your phone or a sunrise alarm clock, because earplugs plus a sound machine will mute a phone speaker. If your ears hurt, itch or feel blocked for more than a day, stop using them and talk to a clinician.

Is it better to sleep in silence or with white noise during the day?

White noise, for most people in a noisy house. True silence does not exist in the daytime — the house still creaks, pipes knock and traffic passes — and in a silent room every small sound stands out and wakes you. A steady masking sound raises the floor so those sounds blend in. The exception is a genuinely quiet house: if the only noise is a distant hum, earplugs alone may be enough. Try earplugs first, add the machine if you still wake to household sounds.

How dark does the bedroom need to be for day sleep?

Dark enough that you cannot see your hand — that is the standard in the CDC/NIOSH guidance for shift workers, and it is a useful test. Blackout curtains do the heavy lifting; a contoured mask such as the Manta Sleep Mask Pro at $68 on sale (list $85) covers what the curtains miss, and tape over LED indicators and a dark or covered clock finish the job.

How do I get my family to be quiet while I sleep in the day?

Make it a schedule, not a request. Put your sleep hours on the fridge or the family calendar, agree a quiet-hours window, and move noisy chores — vacuuming, laundry, DIY — outside it. Give children a concrete job: headphones for videos and games during your sleep, and a small reward for keeping to it. A sign on the bedroom door helps visitors and delivery drivers.
