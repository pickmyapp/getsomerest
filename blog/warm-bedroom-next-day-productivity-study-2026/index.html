Warm Bedroom, Slower Workday? Study (2026)

A 2026 study tracked 82 adults in Austria: warmer-than-usual bedrooms meant lower sleep scores and lower next-day focus. The numbers — and their limits.

Share
9 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Nov 13, 2026 · Last updated Nov 13, 2026

A warm bedroom may cost you more than a restless night. A study published in November 2026 in the Journal of Environmental Psychology followed 82 adults in Austria through the summer of 2025 and found that sleeping in a bedroom warmer than your own usual temperature was linked to slightly lower sleep quality and slightly lower self-reported focus and productivity the next day. For each degree Celsius above a person's average bedroom temperature, the study reports about a half-point drop on a 100-point sleep score and about a 0.04-point dip on a five-point productivity scale. Those are small effects — and the study was observational, so it cannot prove that warm rooms cause the dips. It is one of the first to track all three in real homes, night after night.

Quick answer

Sleeping in a bedroom warmer than your own usual temperature was linked to a small drop in sleep quality and slightly lower self-reported focus and productivity the next day, according to a 2026 study of 82 adults in Austria. What the Study Found

A warm bedroom may be a quiet drag on the next day. The study, “Rising temperatures – deteriorating productivity,” was published in the Journal of Environmental Psychology in November 2026. Led by Christian Rominger of the University of Hildesheim, with colleagues at the University of Graz and the Technical University of Vienna, it tracked 82 adults in Austria at home during July and August 2025, measuring bedroom temperature, sleep and daytime focus night after night.

The headline numbers are small but consistent. When a person's bedroom was warmer than their own usual average, their objective sleep score — a 0-to-100 score calculated by a wearable ring — dropped by about half a point per additional degree Celsius. Warmer nights also went along with worse morning sleep ratings and greater next-day sleepiness. Sleeping in a bedroom warmer than one's personal average also predicted slightly lower self-reported focus and productivity the next day: about 0.04 points on a five-point scale for each additional degree Celsius.

The more interesting finding is which comparison matters. Average bedroom temperatures across different households showed no significant links to overall sleep or productivity. It was the within-person comparison — warmer than your usual room — that predicted the dips. But because each link was tested in a separate analysis, the study does not show that poorer sleep is the route through which warm bedrooms relate to next-day productivity. The chain from warm room to worse sleep to slower workday is plausible, but this study does not prove it.

How the Study Worked

What makes this study different from most bedroom-temperature research is that it happened in real homes, not a climate chamber. This team sent sensors home with people and watched their actual bedrooms through a real summer.

The sample was 82 adults in Austria, aged 20 to 65, with an average age of 28 — 56 women, 24 men and 2 gender-diverse participants. Volunteers with sleep or cardiovascular disorders were excluded. Most worked in offices, and 33 of the 82 had a cooling system at home. Data collection ran in July and August 2025 in Vienna, Graz and nearby suburbs, where outdoor temperatures ranged from about 10 to 37°C (50 to 99°F). After removing sick days and nights away from home, the analysis covered 1,189 paired nights and following days.

Each participant placed an air monitor near bed height to log bedroom temperature, relative humidity and carbon dioxide every five seconds during sleep. Nighttime bedroom temperatures averaged 24.0°C (75.1°F), spanning 18.5 to 28.7°C (65.4 to 83.7°F). Participants wore an Oura Ring, which calculated a 0-to-100 sleep score and estimated sleep efficiency, time to fall asleep, restless periods, deep sleep and REM sleep. Each morning they rated their sleep.

Daytime focus was captured in real time. An Apple Watch recorded heart rate and steps while sending eight prompts between 7:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m., each asking how focused and productive participants felt on a five-point scale. This real-time check-in — called ecological momentary assessment — avoids relying on memory at the end of the day. Participants also rated daytime sleepiness and mood, so the team could test whether warm nights related to focus even after mood was taken into account. One gap: the team had no night-by-night records of fans, windows, air conditioning or bed-sharing.

What Warmer Nights Did to Sleep

The sleep findings are a mix of small but consistent links and clear non-findings, and the non-findings matter as much as the findings. On warmer-than-usual nights, the objective sleep score dipped, morning sleep ratings worsened, and next-day sleepiness rose. Among individual sleep metrics, warmer bedroom nights corresponded with more restless periods and shorter REM sleep — the stage tied to dreaming and memory. A tendency toward shorter deep sleep on warmer nights was not statistically significant.

Equally important is what did not change. Light sleep, time in bed, sleep efficiency (the share of time in bed actually spent asleep) and time to fall asleep had no significant links to bedroom heat. So the picture is not “sleep falls apart when the room is warm.” It is more specific: people tossed and turned more and spent less time in REM, while how long they were in bed, how efficiently they slept and how fast they drifted off stayed much the same.

The REM result deserves a caution flag. The links with individual sleep stages were tested one by one, so the REM finding in particular should be treated cautiously — testing many metrics separately increases the chance that one of them looks significant by luck. The restless-periods finding and the overall sleep-score dip are the sturdier results, because they line up with the morning ratings and the next-day sleepiness, all pointing the same way.

Two side findings show how messy real-bedroom data can be. Bedroom humidity shifts were not significant for sleep or productivity. And nights with higher bedroom carbon dioxide — a marker of stale air — unexpectedly went with slightly higher objective sleep scores — the authors note carbon dioxide stayed relatively low overall, averaging 793 parts per million, and suggest unmeasured factors may explain the pattern: closing a window raises carbon dioxide, but it also blocks outdoor noise.

Warm Bedrooms vs Cooler Ones

Because the study compared each person with themselves, the useful contrast is not “warm rooms versus cold rooms” but “a night warmer than your usual versus a night at or below your usual.” Here is what the study reports for each side of that comparison, and what it measured and what it did not.

That second table is the honest frame for the whole story. The study is strong on measurement — real rooms, real nights, real-time daytime check-ins — and thin on the habits that shape those measurements. A fan can cool the sleeper while the sensor records a warm room; a closed window can raise CO₂ while blocking noise. The authors say as much, and it is why the study reads as a pattern to test further, not a prescription to buy a cooler. Not all recent research points the same way, either: a different 2026 study of 108 adults with type 2 diabetes found warmer-than-usual nights linked to higher sleep efficiency, so the picture is not a settled, one-direction story.

What This Means for Your Bedroom

This study is a report, not a prescription. It does not tell you what temperature to set your bedroom to, and it does not say that a warm room will harm your health. What it does suggest is that nights that run hotter than your own usual room tend to go with slightly less restful sleep and a slightly slower-feeling next day. If that matches your own experience — summer nights where you wake up groggy and the day feels harder — the study gives that experience a measured, small-scale backing.

The most practical step is also the cheapest: find out what your bedroom actually runs at. Most people guess, and guesses are usually wrong by several degrees. A simple bedroom thermometer on the nightstand tells you your own average over a week, which is the number this study says matters. Once you know your baseline, you can notice which nights run above it and whether those mornings feel different — the same within-person comparison the researchers used.

Beyond measuring, the usual comfort levers apply — lighter bedding on warm nights, a fan or open window for airflow, and keeping the room from building up heat during the day. Our guide to sleeping cool without AC covers the no-AC options in detail, and if night sweats are the issue rather than room temperature, our picks for the best cooling mattress toppers and best cooling sheets for night sweats are worth a look. None of these is a treatment for a sleep problem — if warm nights are regularly leaving you exhausted, that is a conversation for a clinician, not a shopping list.

Two caveats should stay attached to any takeaway. First, this was summer data from mid-sized Austrian cities, among a relatively young, mostly office-working sample, tracked for two to three weeks. The authors note that mild heat and gradual adaptation to summer warmth may have muted the effects compared with sudden early-summer heat waves or hotter regions. Second, the effects were small — not the difference between a good day and a ruined one.

How We Reported This Story

We have not read the full peer-reviewed paper, and no independent testing of the findings existed at writing time. Everything here comes from the study as reported in the Journal of Environmental Psychology and summarized by PsyPost on November 12, 2026, checked against the study title, authors and reported figures.

  • Study: “Rising temperatures – deteriorating productivity,” by Christian Rominger and colleagues, Journal of Environmental Psychology, November 2026.
  • Sample: 82 adults in Austria, aged 20 to 65 (average age 28), in Vienna, Graz and nearby suburbs, July–August 2025; 1,189 paired nights and following days.
  • Key figures: about a 0.5-point sleep-score drop per °C above a person's average bedroom temperature; about a 0.04-point productivity dip per °C on a five-point scale; bedroom temperatures averaged 24.0°C (75.1°F), ranging from 18.5 to 28.7°C. All figures as reported in the PsyPost summary.
  • Limits: observational design, small and young sample, summer data only, self-reported productivity rather than objective output, stage-by-stage testing that makes the REM result tentative, and no records of fans, windows, air conditioning or bed-sharing.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a warm bedroom affect sleep, according to the study?

The effect was small. For each degree Celsius a bedroom was warmer than a person's own average, the study reports about a half-point drop on a 100-point sleep score, along with more restless periods and shorter REM sleep. Warmer nights also went with worse morning sleep ratings and greater next-day sleepiness. Sleep efficiency, time to fall asleep, light sleep and time in bed showed no significant link to bedroom heat.

Does a warm bedroom make you less productive at work?

The study found a small association, not proof. Sleeping in a bedroom warmer than one's personal average predicted slightly lower self-reported focus and productivity the next day — about 0.04 points on a five-point scale per additional degree Celsius. That dip held up after adjusting for weekends, prior-day productivity, home cooling systems and next-day mood. But productivity was measured with two self-report items, not objective work output or cognitive tests, and the study was observational, so it cannot show that bedroom heat caused the dip.

What bedroom temperature is best for sleep?

This study does not set an ideal bedroom temperature — it only compares warmer-than-usual nights with a person's own average. Bedroom temperatures in the study averaged 24.0°C (75.1°F). Research more broadly tends to point to a cooler room for comfortable sleep — see our roundup of the research on ideal bedroom temperature for sleep. The practical takeaway here is relative: nights hotter than your own usual room may feel less restful.

Is this study proof that warm bedrooms cause poor sleep?

No. The researchers observed people sleeping naturally at home rather than assigning room temperatures, so the study cannot establish that bedroom heat directly caused the dips in sleep or focus. The links with individual sleep stages were tested one by one, so the REM sleep result in particular should be treated cautiously, and the study does not show that poorer sleep is the route through which warm bedrooms relate to next-day productivity. What it shows is a consistent day-to-day pattern in real homes — a starting point for controlled trials, not a verdict.

How is this study different from the Penn State and Portland studies?

This study followed 82 working-age adults (average age 28) in Vienna and Graz, Austria, in the summer of 2025, combining bedroom air sensors, a wearable sleep ring and smartwatch check-ins eight times a day. Our earlier Penn State study looked at young adults and the combined effects of light, heat and noise; the Portland study focused on older adults. The new study is distinct because it tracked night-to-night changes within the same person — warmer than your own usual room, not warmer than someone else's — and linked those nights to next-day self-reported productivity.

Key Takeaways

  • The finding: in a 2026 study of 82 adults in Austria, bedrooms warmer than a person's own usual temperature were linked to lower sleep scores, more restless periods, shorter REM sleep, and slightly lower next-day focus and productivity.
  • The size of it: about a half-point drop on a 100-point sleep score and about a 0.04-point dip on a five-point productivity scale per degree Celsius above your own average — small effects across 1,189 nights and days.
  • Your baseline matters: average temperatures across households showed no link to sleep or productivity; only warmer-than-usual nights within the same person did.
  • What did not change: sleep efficiency, time to fall asleep and light sleep showed no significant link to bedroom heat, and the deep-sleep tendency was not significant.
  • The limits: observational design, a small and young summer sample (average age 28, two to three weeks in Vienna and Graz), self-reported productivity, and no records of fans, windows, AC or bed-sharing.
  • The practical step: measure your own bedroom temperature over a week to learn your baseline, then notice which nights run above it and how those mornings feel — a comfort check, not a medical one.
