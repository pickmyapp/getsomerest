How to choose latex and memory foam mattresses

In this guide, latex and memory foam mattresses means latex and memory foam mattresses are the two most common foam comfort materials in modern beds, and whichever one sits in your top comfort layer largely decides how warm or cool you sleep. Most buying mistakes come from skipping the same few checks, so run through the list below before you compare prices.

  • Price: compare the real checkout price, including shipping, not just the list price.
  • Materials: look for clear details about fabrics and fill instead of vague marketing terms.
  • Trial period: a longer home trial makes it easier to return something that doesn’t suit you.
  • Warranty and returns: read the terms, including who pays for return shipping.

The short version of the science: memory foam is dense and conforms closely to your body, which limits airflow and lets heat pool at the surface — the classic 'sleeping hot' complaint. Latex has an open-cell structure with tiny pinholes from manufacturing, so air moves through it instead of getting trapped. Pocketed coils underneath either material improve cooling further, because the spaces between coils act as ventilation channels.

The Sleep Foundation recommends keeping your bedroom between 65 and 68°F (18.3–20°C), noting that your body's temperature naturally drops as you sleep — so a cooler room makes it easier to fall and stay asleep.

How latex and memory foam actually handle heat

Think of it as the difference between a sponge and a dense rubber mat. Latex behaves like the sponge: its open cells let warm air escape and cooler air circulate with every small movement you make during the night. Memory foam behaves more like the mat — it absorbs your shape beautifully, but that close contact seals off airflow, so your body heat has nowhere to go. Two mattresses with identical firmness can feel completely different at 2 a.m.

Cooling technologies like gel infusions, graphite and phase-change covers can narrow the gap, and they genuinely help for the first hour or two of sleep. But most infusions can only absorb so much heat; once saturated, the foam behaves like regular memory foam for the rest of the night. The material's underlying breathability matters more than any add-on, which is why latex starts the race with a real advantage.

Frequently asked questions

Does latex really sleep cooler than memory foam?

Yes, in most real-world comparisons. Latex has an open-cell structure with tiny pinholes left over from manufacturing, which lets air circulate through the material instead of getting trapped. Memory foam, by contrast, is denser and conforms closely to your body, which limits airflow and lets body heat build up at the surface — the classic sleeping hot complaint. Independent reviewers consistently rate latex and latex-hybrid mattresses higher for temperature neutrality than all-foam memory foam beds. The difference is biggest in all-latex versus all-memory-foam comparisons; once you add coils to either side, the gap narrows because coils add airflow. No mattress material can overpower a very warm bedroom, though — pair a breathable mattress with a cool room around 65°F and breathable bedding for the full effect.

Is a latex hybrid or an all-latex mattress cooler?

A latex hybrid usually sleeps a touch cooler than an all-latex mattress, and the reason is airflow. In a hybrid, the comfort layer of latex sits on top of pocketed coils, and the spaces between those coils act as ventilation channels that let warm air escape downward and out the sides of the bed. An all-latex mattress is still far more breathable than memory foam, but it is a solid stack of foam with no air gaps, so it retains slightly more heat. Hybrids also tend to cost less than thick all-latex builds and offer better edge support. The one case where all-latex wins is durability and motion isolation — but for pure cooling, a hybrid is the safer bet.

Do gel infusions and cooling covers actually make memory foam cooler?

They help, but with limits. Gel beads, graphite, copper and phase-change materials are added to memory foam to absorb heat or move it away from your body, and cooling covers use breathable or moisture-wicking fabrics. In testing, these features usually make the first hour or two of sleep feel noticeably cooler — the cool-to-the-touch effect you notice when you first lie down. The catch is that most infusions have a limited capacity to absorb heat; once saturated, the foam behaves like regular memory foam for the rest of the night. Phase-change covers tend to perform better over a full night than gel infusions do. Think of cooling tech as a useful boost, not a fix — it works best on a mattress that already breathes reasonably well.

Will a cooling mattress topper fix my hot memory foam mattress?

A cooling topper can meaningfully improve a hot mattress, and it is the cheapest fix worth trying before replacing the bed. A breathable latex or wool topper adds a ventilated layer between you and the heat-trapping foam, which is exactly where most of the warmth builds up. Our separate guide to the best cooling mattress toppers for night sweats compares the options that work best for this. Set expectations, though: a topper cannot add the deep airflow that coils provide, so a thick all-foam mattress will still sleep warmer than a hybrid. If your mattress is also sagging or more than eight years old, the topper is a band-aid — put the money toward a new breathable mattress instead.

Which lasts longer, latex or memory foam?

Latex wins on durability by a clear margin. A quality natural latex mattress typically lasts 12 to 15 years or more, while most memory foam mattresses start to soften and develop body impressions after 7 to 10 years. Latex is naturally more resilient — it springs back instead of slowly conforming — so it resists the sagging that ends a memory foam bed's useful life. That longevity is part of why latex mattresses cost more upfront; spread over 12-plus years, the yearly cost often ends up similar. Whichever you choose, a breathable mattress protector and a supportive foundation will extend its life. And durability ties back to cooling: a sagging foam mattress traps more heat because your body sinks deeper into warm foam.
