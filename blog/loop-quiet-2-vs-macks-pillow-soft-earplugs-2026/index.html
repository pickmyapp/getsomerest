How to read earplug ratings (before you buy)

Earplug boxes shout decibel numbers that are easy to misread. Here is the short version: SNR (Single Number Rating) is the European lab standard; NRR (Noise Reduction Rating) is the US EPA standard. They use different test methodologies, so an SNR number and an NRR number are not directly comparable — claiming the Loop's 24 dB SNR beats Mack's 22 dB NRR by 2 dB would be wrong. Loop's own US NRR is 14 dB.

What the numbers mean in a bedroom. Both plugs sit in the mid-range: enough to take the edge off typical snoring and street traffic, not enough for an extremely loud snorer or construction next door. Independent lab testing (HearingTracker) puts the Loop Quiet 2 at 24 dB SNR, consistent with Loop's claim. For perspective, no passive earplug gives you silence — they give you quieter.

Fit philosophy is the real decision. The Loop inserts a soft tip into the ear canal with an acoustic channel that lowers volume evenly — you can still hear your alarm. Mack's putty molds over the ear opening without entering the canal at all, which sensitive ears prefer, and it doubles as a waterproof seal for swimming and showering. Reusable precision vs disposable simplicity: pick the one your ears will tolerate nightly, because the best earplug is the one you actually wear.

Our verdict: which earplugs to buy

Buy the Loop Quiet 2 if you wear earplugs nightly. One $24.95 purchase, washable silicone, four tip sizes for a custom fit, a keychain carry case, and a 100-day risk-free trial — it pays for itself in about three months of nightly use and generates no plastic waste. The acoustic channel keeps alarm audibility, which nightly users care about.

Buy Mack's Pillow Soft if you wear earplugs occasionally, have sensitive ears, or swim. It is also the travel-kit and swim-bag pick: waterproof seal included.

Many of our readers end up owning both: the Loop on the nightstand for nightly use, a box of Mack's in the travel bag and bathroom drawer. At these prices, that is not indulgence — it is a system.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Loop Quiet 2's 24 dB rating better than Mack's 22 dB?

Not directly comparable — and this matters. Loop's 24 dB is an SNR (the European lab standard); Mack's 22 dB is an NRR (the US EPA lab standard). Different test methodologies produce different numbers, so '2 dB quieter' would be a false claim. Loop's own US NRR rating is 14 dB. Both are mid-range reductions: enough to take the edge off moderate snoring and traffic, not enough for extremely loud snorers.

Are reusable earplugs more hygienic than disposable ones?

They can be, because you wash them. The Loop Quiet 2 is washable and reusable, so wax and dust do not accumulate the way they do on a disposable plug you keep reusing past its life. Mack's putty is rated for about 5 uses per pair — past that, it stops being tacky and should be replaced. The hygiene rule for both: follow the use limits and wash your hands before handling them.

Can side sleepers wear the Loop Quiet 2 comfortably?

Most can: the Loop's low-profile silicone ring sits mostly outside the ear with a small tip in the canal, so it does not jab the pillow the way foam cylinders do. That said, Loop does not publish a side-sleeper-specific comfort claim, and ears vary. Mack's has the edge for sensitive ears: the putty molds flat over the ear opening with nothing inside the canal at all, which third-party reviewers rate well for side sleeping.

How long does one box of Mack's Pillow Soft last?

Each pair is rated for about 5 uses, so a 6-pair box (~$8) covers roughly 30 nights — about $0.27 per night. That math flips the comparison on its head: Mack's is the cheaper try-it-tonight buy, but at nightly use the $24.95 Loop Quiet 2 pays for itself after roughly three months. Buy Mack's for occasional use and travel kits; buy the Loop for nightly wear.

Do earplugs or white noise work better for a snoring partner?

They solve different problems, and many couples use both. Earplugs reduce the sound that reaches you; white noise masks the sound that gets through. For moderate snoring, earplugs alone often suffice. For loud, variable snoring, layering helps: earplugs plus a white-noise machine covers what either one misses.
