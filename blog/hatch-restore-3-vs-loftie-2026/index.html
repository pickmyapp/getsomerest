Sunrise light or two-phase alarm: how to pick

These two clocks solve the same problem — a phone alarm that wrecks your wind-down and your wake-up — from opposite directions. The Restore 3 assumes light will ease you in and out of sleep; the Loftie assumes the alarm itself is the weak link, and fixes it with a two-stage wake sequence. Neither is a medical device, and neither will fix a broken sleep schedule on its own; if your bedtime is drifting, start with a plan for fixing your sleep schedule and let the clock support it.

  • Waking up is the pain point: if you sleep through alarms, the Loftie's two-phase sequence gives you a second chance built into the design — a gentle tone that fades, then a firmer get-up alarm nine minutes later.
  • Falling asleep is the pain point: if you toss and turn because your brain is still wired, the Restore 3's wind-down routine with sunset-style dimming gives the evening structure. Pair it with a 30-minute evening routine.
  • You want the phone gone entirely: the Loftie's no-WiFi mode goes further than the Restore 3's app-based setup. If this is the real goal, read our 7-day phone-free bedroom challenge before you buy.
  • You need a speaker too: only the Loftie doubles as a Bluetooth speaker. The Restore 3 is not one.

The Restore 3 is the pick for people who buy into the ritual — the evening routine and the dawn simulation as a pair. If you only want an alarm that works, the fancier clock won't help.

Light-first or sound-first?

Dawn simulation works by starting light early — about 30 minutes before your alarm on the Restore 3 — so your room brightens gradually instead of a phone blaring into darkness. It suits people who wake up groggy and want the environment to do some of the lifting. The Loftie takes the opposite bet: no sunrise at all, just a soft amber night light, and all the engineering goes into the alarm sequence. If you've ever slept through a gentle sunrise lamp, the Loftie's second get-up alarm is the more honest answer. See also our sunrise clocks for heavy sleepers and the new sunrise clocks released this year.

Do the subscription math

Both clocks are generous out of the box: the Restore 3 includes 40 sleep sounds, 20 alarm sounds, and 20 lighting options with no subscription, and the Loftie includes 100+ sounds and soundscapes free. The paid tiers — Hatch+ at roughly $5 a month (30-day free trial) and Loftie Premium at $36 a year — only unlock the expanded libraries. If you mostly use one or two favorite sounds, you may never need to pay.

How phone-free is your goal?

Both brands market a phone-free bedroom, but the depth differs. The Restore 3 needs the Hatch Sleep app and 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi for setup and scheduling — daily use then runs from its physical buttons and rotating control. The Loftie is designed to work without Wi-Fi at all, and its blackout mode removes even the glowing display. If the goal is a genuinely phone-free room, the Loftie asks less of your phone.

Our verdict: which $170 clock wins your nightstand

The narrow winner is the Loftie — but only by a whisker, and only for a specific kind of buyer. The two-phase alarm is the single most useful idea either clock offers: a gentle wake tone that fades, followed nine minutes later by a second get-up alarm, is a genuinely better answer to oversleeping than either a single tone or a sunrise you can sleep through. Add the 5-hour backup battery, the Bluetooth speaker, and the no-WiFi mode, and the Loftie is the more complete phone-free bedroom replacement for the money.

That said, the Restore 3 is the better clock if you're light-sensitive or routine-driven. Dawn simulation that starts 30 minutes before your alarm, a guided wind-down with sunset-style dimming, and ~4.2 stars across thousands of Amazon ratings make it the proven pick for people who wake up groggy and fall asleep wired. If sunrise lamps have worked for you before, the Restore 3 is a straight upgrade.

Who should ignore our pick? Sunrise loyalists and heavy sleepers who respond to light — the Loftie has no sunrise simulation at all, just an amber night light. And if the evening matters as much as the morning, the Restore 3's wind-down routine is the stronger half of the day. Buy for the mechanism you trust: light-first and routine → Restore 3; sound-first and phone-free → Loftie.

Frequently asked questions

Are heated mattress pads safe to leave on all night?

Yes, with a modern pad that has an auto shut-off timer and overheat protection, such as the ETL- or UL-certified models we compare here. Set the auto-off timer for the time you actually need — most sleepers use one or two hours after falling asleep rather than the full 12-hour window — and never use a pad that is damp or folded over itself.

Heated mattress pad vs electric blanket: which is better for sleep?

A heated mattress pad usually wins for sleep. It warms the bed from underneath so heat rises around you, it never slides off, and it can preheat the bed before you get in. Electric blankets trap heat on top and often make sleepers overheat; a pad plus your normal blanket lets you fine-tune warmth on each side.

How much does it cost to run a heated mattress pad?

Very little. A pad like the Bwarm HeatSync draws about 65 watts per side — roughly the same as an incandescent light bulb. Running one side for eight hours costs only a few cents of electricity, far less than running a 1,500-watt space heater to warm the whole bedroom.

Will a heated mattress pad fit my mattress?

Most fit mattresses up to 18 inches deep, which covers the majority of pillow-top and hybrid mattresses. If your mattress is thicker than 18 inches, pick a deep-pocket model — the Beautyrest Cotton pad here fits up to 21 inches, and all three pads use all-around elastic skirts so they stay put.

Can I wash a heated mattress pad?

Yes — all three pads we compare are machine washable. Detach the controller and cords first, wash on a gentle cycle, and make sure the pad is completely dry before plugging it back in. Never dry clean or bleach it, and never operate a pad that is still damp.
