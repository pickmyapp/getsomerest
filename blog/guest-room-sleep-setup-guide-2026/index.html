How to Choose Each Piece

A guest room has one job: help a tired person fall asleep fast in a strange house. Every buying decision should serve that job, in this order.

The bed: height matters more than softness. Low air mattresses — the kind that sit six inches off the floor — are fine for camping but miserable for guests, especially anyone over 40. A 20-inch raised queen air mattress sleeps at normal bed height, fits standard queen sheets, and feels like a bed instead of a floor pad. Built-in pump is non-negotiable: it inflates in about two minutes with no hunting for accessories at midnight. The honest tradeoff of any air mattress is slow leak-down over hours; a quilted top and coil construction resist it, and the best defense is simply buying a well-reviewed model rather than the cheapest one. If your guests visit often or you want to skip the air mattress entirely, a tri-fold memory foam mattress (Twin ~$150-$200, Queen ~$280-$350) folds into a closet and never deflates — see the price row in our comparison table.

The bedding: clean-looking beats thread count. Guests judge the room in the first five seconds, and crisp, coordinated sheets are what register. Budget microfiber sets like the Mellanni look fresh out of the package, resist wrinkles, and have deep pockets that stay on tall air mattresses — exactly what the category is for. Purists will note microfiber is polyester: it sleeps warmer than cotton. For a guest bed used a few dozen nights a year, that tradeoff is invisible; for hot climates, pair it with a cotton blanket as the top layer. And buy a second pillow set: flat, dead pillows are the most common complaint about guest beds, and a $40 two-pack of down-alternative pillows fixes it outright. Our pillow guide covers the details.

The sound: masking, not silence. Your house makes unfamiliar noises at night — the fridge, the heating, your 6 a.m. alarm — and unfamiliar noises are exactly what wake guests up. A white noise machine lays down a steady sound floor so those spikes never register. Budget digital machines (the Dreamegg) win for guest rooms because they are simple: press one button, no app, no account, rechargeable so there is no cable clutter. Real-fan machines like the Yogasleep Dohm produce the most natural sound, but they are plug-in only and cost twice as much. Pick digital for simplicity, fan-based for sound purists.

Pairs well with: the free layer in the next sections — blackout on the brightest window, a spare blanket, water and a charger on the nightstand — plus our blackout curtain guide if you want a permanent solution for the spare room.

Frequently asked questions

Do I really need to buy anything for the guest room, or is a couch fine?

You can get away with the free basics — blackout approach, a spare blanket, a glass of water, and a charging cable — and your guest will sleep noticeably better than on a bare couch. But a couch is a compromise your guest feels in their back the next morning. The ~$170 trio we recommend (queen air mattress, sheet set, white noise machine) costs less than one hotel night and upgrades every future visit. If you host more than twice a year, it pays for itself in saved apologies.

Are air mattresses actually comfortable for adults?

The tall ones are. A 20-inch raised queen air mattress with a quilted top and coil construction sleeps at real-bed height and does not fold your guests into a taco the way thin camping pads do. The catch: cheap air mattresses leak down overnight, so pick a well-reviewed model with a built-in pump like the King Koil. And always make it up with a fitted sheet — fabric against skin hides the plastic feel completely. For fussy sleepers, a tri-fold memory foam mattress (~$150-$220 in twin) skips the leak-down problem entirely.

How do I make a spare room dark without blackout curtains?

Layering beats spending. Heavy existing curtains plus a draft-stopper-style door gap towel plus aluminum foil taped to the window (shiny side out) will darken a room dramatically for pennies. Our full comparison lives in our blackout curtain guide, but for a guest room the 80/20 fix is free: foil or a cheap suction blackout blind on the single brightest window, and a sleep mask in the welcome basket as backup.

Should I leave a white noise machine in the guest room, or is it overkill?

It is one of the highest-leverage items you can add. Guests sleep in an unfamiliar house full of unfamiliar sounds — the heating clicking on, your early alarm, the dog — and a steady noise floor masks all of it. A $21 machine like the Dreamegg D11 Max sits on the nightstand, needs no app or account, and works for every age. Leave it pre-set to plain white noise at medium volume; guests can explore the other 20 sounds if they want.

What goes in a guest welcome basket?

Keep it simple: bottled or filtered water, a phone charger with a long cable, earplugs, a sleep mask, a spare toothbrush and toothpaste, the Wi-Fi password on a card, and a spare blanket folded at the foot of the bed. Everything here costs under $20 total if you buy the basics in multipacks, and it reads as genuinely thoughtful rather than performative. The basket lives in the closet between visits and comes out in 30 seconds.
