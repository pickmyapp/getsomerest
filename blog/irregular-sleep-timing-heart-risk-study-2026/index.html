Irregular Sleep Timing Tied to Higher Heart Risk (2026)

Irregular bedtimes were linked to 20–23% higher heart risk in a 10,362-person study — each 29 minutes of variation added 24%. What it means, and the limits.

Share
8 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Nov 10, 2026 · Last updated Nov 10, 2026

How regular your bedtime is may matter as much as how long you sleep. New research presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions 2026 found that people whose sleep timing swung from night to night faced a 20–23% higher risk of serious heart events — heart attack, stroke and heart failure — than people with steadier schedules. The study is large, with more than one million nights of wearable data from 10,362 adults, but it is also preliminary and observational: it shows a link, not proof that irregular sleep causes heart disease. Here is what the researchers actually measured, what the numbers mean in plain terms, and where the finding fits into a sensible sleep routine.

Quick answer

The short answer: In a 10,362-person study, night-to-night swings in bedtime and wake time were linked to a 20–23% higher risk of serious heart events. Each 29 minutes of extra variation in sleep length added 24% to the combined risk. The study is observational and preliminary — it shows an association, not proof — but a steady sleep window costs nothing to try.

At a Glance: What the Study Found

Your bedtime may matter as much as your sleep length — at least, that is what the largest wearable-based study of sleep regularity so far suggests. Researchers led by Nguyen Minh Tuan Viet of Boston Children's Hospital and Harvard Medical School analyzed more than one million nights of Fitbit data from 10,362 adults in the NIH All of Us Research Program, then followed them for a median of 3.7 years to see who developed heart problems.

  • 20–23% higher risk: people whose sleep timing varied the most from night to night had a 20–23% higher risk of serious cardiovascular events — heart attack, stroke and heart failure — than the steadiest sleepers.
  • 29 minutes is the unit that matters: each additional 29 minutes of night-to-night variation in sleep length was associated with a 24% higher combined cardiovascular risk, a 29% higher risk of cardiomyopathy and a 20% higher risk of heart failure.
  • 55% for long sleep cycles: an unusually long sleep cycle was linked to a 55% higher risk of heart attack or stroke.
  • Big, objective data: 1,017,812 nights from wrist-worn Fitbits over a roughly six-month baseline — far more reliable than sleep diaries, which is what most older regularity studies relied on.
  • But preliminary: the work is a conference abstract presented at AHA Scientific Sessions 2026 (November 6–9, Chicago). It has not been peer-reviewed, and as an observational study it cannot prove that irregular sleep causes heart disease.

The study's real contribution is shifting the question. Sleep research has spent decades asking how long people sleep; this one asks how steadily they sleep — and finds that steadiness carries its own signal, separate from duration.

How to Read This Study Without Overreading It

Three words should frame everything that follows: preliminary, observational, association. The findings were presented as a conference abstract, and abstracts at AHA meetings are not peer-reviewed — the association treats them as tentative until they appear as a full paper in a journal. The study is observational, which means it can show that irregular sleep and heart events travel together, not that one causes the other. And the numbers are relative risks: a 20–23% higher risk describes the increase relative to the steadiest sleepers in this particular group, not an absolute chance for any one person.

That does not make the study weak. A sample of 10,362 people with more than a million nights of objective wearable data is far stronger than the diary-based studies that used to dominate sleep research, and the six-month baseline window means the regularity measure reflects a real habit rather than one bad week. But the right way to use it is as a nudge toward a steadier routine, not as a new health scare. The AHA's own volunteer expert, Marie-Pierre St-Onge — who chaired the association's 2025 scientific statement on sleep and heart health — said the findings should be interpreted cautiously, and that further research is needed to determine whether improving sleep regularity can independently reduce cardiovascular risk.

What counts as irregular here: the researchers measured how much three things varied from night to night over about six months — bedtime, wake time, and total sleep length. People in the most irregular group were not necessarily short sleepers; the risk showed up even after the analysis accounted for how long people slept. That is the study's most interesting point: consistency itself, separate from duration, was the signal.

What the study did not test: nobody was asked to fix their schedule and then followed to see whether their risk fell. No intervention was tested at all. So the honest reading is that steady sleepers had fewer heart events in this dataset — and the reason could be the schedule itself, or something that travels with it, such as shift work, stress, or other health habits that make both sleep and heart risk worse.

Why the 29-minute figure matters: it turns an abstract idea — irregularity — into something measurable. Most people can estimate whether their sleep length swings by 15 minutes or by an hour from night to night, and the study suggests the difference between those two is real. It also hints at why shift workers and new parents show up so often in sleep research: their schedules vary by hours, not minutes, which is exactly the direction this risk points.

Our Verdict: The Honest Takeaway

The finding is worth taking seriously, and worth not panicking about. A 20–23% relative increase in serious heart events, in a sample of more than 10,000 people tracked with wearables for over a million nights, is not a rounding error — and it lines up with a growing body of work showing that sleep regularity, not just sleep length, tracks with health. The 29-minute figure is the most usable part: every extra 29 minutes of night-to-night variation in sleep length was tied to a 24% higher combined risk, which gives a concrete sense of scale. A schedule that drifts by half an hour from night to night is, in this dataset, meaningfully different from one that drifts by ten minutes.

But the causal chain is unproven. Nothing in this study shows that forcing yourself onto a rigid schedule will lower your heart risk, and the authors do not claim otherwise. The people with the steadiest sleep may also have steadier jobs, steadier meals, less stress and better access to care — all of which protect the heart through their own routes. Until a trial tests whether regularizing sleep actually changes outcomes, the finding is a correlation with a plausible mechanism, not a prescription.

What to do with it: if your bedtime already drifts by an hour or more from night to night, tightening the window is a free, low-risk experiment that sleep researchers recommend anyway for better sleep quality. Pick a wake time you can hold seven days a week, anchor bedtime to it, and let the schedule settle over a few weeks rather than forcing it in one night. If you work shifts or care for a newborn, a perfectly regular schedule may not be realistic — and this study is not a reason to feel guilty about that. It is a reason to protect the regularity you can control, and to talk to a clinician about heart risk factors you can actually measure, such as blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar.

What the Coverage Got Right — and Where It Overreached

Most coverage got the core numbers right. Medical Xpress, Nautilus and the Seoul Economic Daily all reported the 20–23% figure, the 10,362-person sample, the million-plus nights of Fitbit data and the 3.7-year median follow-up, and all three carried the observational caveat prominently. The AHA's own release is the cleanest source: it states the association plainly and quotes the expert caution in the same breath as the headline numbers.

The overreach to watch for is the weekend lie-in framing. Several headlines frame the study as proof that sleeping in on weekends harms your heart. That is an oversimplification: the study measured variation across roughly six months of nights, not Saturday mornings specifically, and it cannot separate weekend lie-ins from every other source of drift — late work nights, social evenings, travel, stress.

The 55% figure needs the same care. An unusually long sleep cycle was linked to a 55% higher risk of heart attack or stroke, and that number has travelled widely because it is dramatic. But it describes a specific pattern — unusually long sleep cycles — in a subset of the data, and it carries the same observational limits as the rest of the study. It is not a claim that sleeping long is dangerous in general; the study's main message is about variation, not duration.

What almost nobody reported: the study's own context. This is the same research program — NIH All of Us — that has quietly become one of the largest sources of wearable sleep data in the world, and the same meeting, AHA Scientific Sessions 2026, also carried the Tulane finding that nighttime light above 3 lux was linked to heart remodeling. Together they point in the same direction: the conditions around sleep, not just its length, are where the new research is looking.

Frequently asked questions

What did the irregular sleep study actually find?

People whose bedtimes and wake times varied the most from night to night had a 20–23% higher risk of serious cardiovascular events — heart attack, stroke, heart failure and heart-muscle disease — than people with steadier schedules. Each 29-minute increase in night-to-night variation in sleep length was tied to a 24% higher combined risk, a 29% higher risk of cardiomyopathy and a 20% higher risk of heart failure. An unusually long sleep cycle was linked to a 55% higher risk of heart attack or stroke.

How big was the study and how was sleep measured?

The analysis used 1,017,812 nights of sleep data from 10,362 adults in the NIH All of Us Research Program, recorded by wrist-worn Fitbit devices over a roughly six-month baseline window. Participants were then followed for a median of 3.7 years for cardiovascular events. The study was led by Nguyen Minh Tuan Viet of Boston Children's Hospital and Harvard Medical School and presented as abstract MDP2618 at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago, November 6–9, 2026.

Does irregular sleep cause heart disease?

The study cannot say that. It is observational, so it shows an association between irregular sleep timing and later heart events, not that erratic sleep directly causes them. It is also preliminary: conference abstracts at AHA meetings are not peer-reviewed, and the findings are considered tentative until published as a full paper. The researchers and the AHA's own expert both stress that improving sleep regularity has not yet been shown to reduce heart risk on its own.

Is sleeping in on weekends the problem?

Weekend lie-ins are one common source of the variation the study measured, but the analysis looked at night-to-night timing shifts across about six months, not weekends alone. The risk was tied to how much bedtimes, wake times and sleep length swung from one night to the next overall — so a regular schedule that shifts by an hour or two every few days counts the same way a weekend lie-in does.

What can I actually do with this finding?

Treat it as a reason to value a steady sleep window, not as a diagnosis. Keeping roughly the same bedtime and wake time — including on weekends — is a low-effort routine change that costs nothing and lines up with what sleep researchers already recommend for better sleep quality. If you have heart concerns, a family history of heart disease, or symptoms such as chest pain or unusual breathlessness, talk to a clinician rather than treating a sleep schedule as a treatment.

Key Takeaways

  • People with the most irregular sleep timing had a 20–23% higher risk of serious heart events in a 10,362-person study presented at AHA Scientific Sessions 2026.
  • Each 29 minutes of extra night-to-night variation in sleep length was tied to a 24% higher combined heart risk, 29% higher cardiomyopathy risk and 20% higher heart-failure risk.
  • An unusually long sleep cycle was linked to a 55% higher risk of heart attack or stroke — a striking but narrow finding in a subset of the data.
  • The study used 1,017,812 nights of Fitbit data from the NIH All of Us program, with a six-month baseline and a median 3.7 years of follow-up.
  • It is observational and preliminary — a conference abstract, not peer-reviewed — so it shows an association, not proof that irregular sleep causes heart disease.
  • The practical takeaway is a steady sleep window, not a scare: hold a consistent wake time, anchor bedtime to it, and talk to a clinician about measurable heart risk factors.
