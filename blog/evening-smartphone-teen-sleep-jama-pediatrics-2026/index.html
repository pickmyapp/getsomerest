Evening Smartphone Use After 10 PM and Teen Sleep: What the 2026 JAMA Pediatrics Study Found

A JAMA Pediatrics study of 425 teens across 7,600 nights found phone use after 10 pm linked to 20 minutes less sleep and 64 minutes later sleep onset. What it measured, and what it did not prove.

Share

By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Oct 26, 2026 · Last updated Oct 26, 2026

The most persuasive study yet on teens, phones, and sleep does not ask teenagers how much they scroll. It watches their phones and their sleep trackers instead. Published in JAMA Pediatrics on October 19, 2026, the study followed 425 teenagers across nearly 7,600 nights, pairing passively sensed phone use with Fitbit-measured sleep. The headline is stark: on nights when a teen used their phone more than their own usual amount after 10 pm, they fell asleep 64 minutes later and slept 20 minutes less. But the study's real value is in its details — the dose-response curve, the last-five-minutes effect, and what it cannot prove. Here is what the researchers measured, what the numbers mean, and what families should actually do with them.

Quick answer

The short answer: in a JAMA Pediatrics study of 425 teens across nearly 7,600 nights, phone use above a teen's own average after 10 pm was linked to falling asleep 64 minutes later and sleeping 20 minutes less. Each extra minute after 10 pm added about 0.87 minutes to sleep onset. Use in the final minutes before sleep was tied to more nighttime wakefulness. The study shows strong associations from passively tracked data — not proof that phones alone cause poor sleep.

At a Glance: What the Study Found

Published in JAMA Pediatrics on October 19, 2026 (Zhao, Hale & Christakis), the study is an intensive longitudinal cohort analysis drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study — the largest long-term study of brain development and child health in the US. It is also a methodological step up from most screen-time research, because it measures both sides of the equation objectively.

  • The setup: 425 US teens (average age just under 15), Android users with at least 14 days of concurrent phone-sensing and Fitbit sleep data, nearly 7,600 nights collected between November 2020 and February 2024.
  • What it found: on nights with above-average phone use after 10 pm, teens fell asleep 64.73 minutes later and slept 20.05 minutes less; each extra minute after 10 pm was linked to onset 0.87 minutes later and total sleep 0.43 minutes shorter.
  • The timing detail: phone use in the last 5 minutes before sleep was linked to the most nighttime wakefulness, concentrated in the first 30 minutes after falling asleep.
  • Why it is stronger than past studies: within-person comparisons (each teen vs themselves), passively sensed phone use, and objectively measured sleep — no self-reported screen time anywhere in the analysis.
  • The honest read: a strong, dose-dependent association between late-evening phone use and poorer same-night sleep — and a practical case for a 10 pm cutoff, not proof that phones alone cause teen sleep problems.

The study's authors put it carefully: reducing evening smartphone use, particularly after 10 pm and immediately before bedtime, may help improve sleep health in adolescents. 'May help' is doing real work in that sentence — and it is the right level of confidence for this evidence.

How to Read This Study Without Overreading It

Screen-time research is easy to misread in both directions — as proof that phones ruin sleep, or as proof that they do not. Three questions keep this study honest.

First, what kind of comparison is it? The strongest kind short of a randomized trial. The researchers used within-person analysis: each teen's heavy-phone nights were compared against that same teen's lighter-phone nights. That design strips out everything stable about a person — personality, school, family rules, baseline sleep — and isolates what changed night to night. When a study compares heavy users against light users instead, those stable differences contaminate everything. This study mostly avoids that trap.

Second, what did it actually measure? Real behavior, not self-reports. Phone use was passively sensed from Android devices through a research app, and sleep was measured by Fitbit — not by asking teens how they slept. That matters because self-reported screen time is unreliable in both directions: heavy users underreport, and tired teens over-attribute. The trade-off is that the sample is Android users in the ABCD Study with at least 14 days of concurrent phone and Fitbit data, which is not every teenager.

Third, what can it not show? Causation, and the direction of it. A teen who cannot fall asleep may reach for their phone because they are awake — and that would produce exactly the same association. The researchers' dose-response finding (more minutes, later sleep) and the timing finding (use closest to sleep onset linked to the most wakefulness) both argue against pure reverse causation, but they do not eliminate it. The honest summary is: late-evening phone use is strongly linked to poorer sleep on the same night, and reducing it is a reasonable, low-cost experiment — not a proven cure.

Our Verdict: The Honest Takeaway

Here is the most defensible reading of this research — what is supported, what is plausible, and what it does not show.

Supported: late-evening phone use and poorer same-night sleep travel together, strongly. A within-person design, passively sensed phone data, objectively measured sleep, nearly 7,600 nights, and a clean dose-response curve add up to the best evidence yet that the link is real and not a self-report artifact. The 10 pm cutoff is not arbitrary: the researchers tested cutoffs from 8 pm to midnight, and the association grew steadily stronger as the cutoff moved later.

Plausible but not proven: phones cause some of the lost sleep. Two mechanisms make sense. Displacement: time on a phone is time not asleep, and teens simply stay up later. And stimulation: screens keep the brain alert and light exposure interferes with the wind-down process, which fits the finding that use in the final minutes before sleep was linked to the most wakefulness. Both mechanisms are consistent with the data; neither is proven by it.

Not shown: that banning phones fixes teen sleep, or that phones are the main cause of it. School start times, homework load, stress, caffeine, and bedroom environment all shape teen sleep, and this study does not rank them. A phone cutoff is a reasonable, reversible experiment — try it for two weeks and see — but it is not a substitute for the bigger levers, and it is certainly not a diagnosis.

What the Coverage Got Right — and Where It Overreached

HealthDay's October 21, 2026 report, carried widely, got the core numbers right: 425 teens, average age nearly 15, nearly 7,600 nights, 20 minutes less sleep and 64 minutes later onset on above-average phone nights after 10 pm. It also correctly quoted the study's own conclusion — that reducing evening phone use, particularly after 10 pm and immediately before bedtime, may help improve sleep health in adolescents — and its lead author, Dr. Dimitri Christakis of Seattle Children's Research Institute.

Independent clinical commentary added the useful context. Dr. Daniel Bigman, a child and adolescent psychiatrist and sleep medicine physician at Northwell Health, noted that the study showed bedtime being pushed later and the transition into stable sleep becoming harder, and offered the two mechanisms — displacement and stimulation — that best explain the pattern. That framing is the right one: it treats the finding as a mechanism to test, not a verdict to repeat.

Where some coverage overreached was in the headline verbs: phones 'wrecking' or 'destroying' teen sleep. The study's language is association, and its effect sizes, while meaningful, are modest in absolute terms — 20 minutes of lost sleep on a heavy-phone night, not hours. A 20-minute nightly loss across a school week adds up to over an hour and a half, which is genuinely worth addressing, but it is not the same as the catastrophic framing some headlines used. The study also belongs to a family of ABCD-based findings: a separate 2026 analysis of 657 teens found average nighttime phone use of over 50 minutes between 10 pm and 6 am on school nights, with over half using devices between midnight and 4 am. The two studies tell the same story from different angles — late-night use is common, and it tracks with poorer sleep.

Frequently asked questions

What did the JAMA Pediatrics study find about teens and phones?

The study, published October 19, 2026, tracked 425 US teenagers (average age just under 15) across nearly 7,600 nights, combining passively sensed phone use with Fitbit-measured sleep. On nights when a teen used their phone more than their own usual amount after 10 pm, they fell asleep an average of 64.73 minutes later and slept 20.05 minutes less. Each extra minute of phone use after 10 pm above a teen's own average was linked to sleep onset arriving about 0.87 minutes later and total sleep shrinking by about 0.43 minutes. Phone use in the final minutes before sleep was linked to more wakefulness during the night, mainly in the first 30 minutes after falling asleep.

How was the study different from earlier screen-time research?

It did not rely on self-reports. Earlier screen-time studies mostly asked teens how much they used their phones — estimates that are notoriously unreliable. This study, led by researchers at Seattle Children's Research Institute with colleagues at Columbia University and Stony Brook University, used passively sensed data from Android phones (through the Effortless Assessment Research System app) and Fitbit sleep measurements, drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. Data were collected between November 2020 and February 2024 and analyzed in 2026. The within-person design — comparing each teen's heavy-phone nights against their own lighter-phone nights — is much stronger than comparing heavy users against light users, because it controls for everything stable about each teen.

Does phone use after 10 pm cause poor sleep?

The study shows association, not proof of cause — and the distinction matters. It is plausible that phone use displaces sleep (teens stay up later because they are on their phones) and that screens stimulate the brain when it should be winding down, as the study's clinical commentators noted. But it is also possible that on nights when a teen cannot sleep, they reach for their phone — reverse causation that a study like this cannot fully rule out. The dose-response pattern (more minutes, later sleep) and the timing pattern (use closest to sleep onset linked to the most wakefulness) both point toward phone use contributing, but neither proves it alone.

Why is 10 pm the cutoff in this study?

Because the researchers tested every hourly cutoff from 8 pm to midnight, and the link between phone use and poorer sleep grew steadily stronger as the cutoff moved later. 10 pm is where the effect became substantial — and it is a practical boundary for families, since it matches a common household rule. The study also found that use in the last 5 minutes before falling asleep was especially tied to nighttime wakefulness, while use in the last 20 minutes showed a minimal effect — suggesting that the final minutes before sleep matter disproportionately.

What should families actually do with this finding?

Treat it as a timing experiment, not a lecture. The study's own conclusion is modest: reducing evening phone use, particularly after 10 pm and immediately before bedtime, may help improve sleep health in adolescents. Practical steps that follow from the data: charge the phone outside the bedroom, set a household cutoff around 10 pm (or earlier on school nights), and replace the last 30 minutes before bed with a non-screen wind-down — reading, stretching, or simply dimming the lights. If a teen's sleep problems persist despite a consistent routine, that is a conversation for a clinician, not a phone setting.

Key Takeaways

  • A JAMA Pediatrics study (Oct 19, 2026) tracked 425 teens across nearly 7,600 nights using passively sensed phone data and Fitbit sleep measurements — not self-reports.
  • On nights with above-average phone use after 10 pm, teens fell asleep 64 minutes later and slept 20 minutes less; each extra minute after 10 pm added ~0.87 minutes to sleep onset.
  • Phone use in the final minutes before sleep was linked to more wakefulness during the night, mainly in the first 30 minutes after falling asleep.
  • The link grew stronger as the cutoff moved from 8 pm toward midnight — 10 pm is a practical boundary, not a biological cliff.
  • The study shows strong associations, not proven causation; a phone cutoff after 10 pm is a reasonable, low-cost experiment, not a cure — and persistent teen sleep problems deserve a clinician's input.
