Ideal Weight for Weighted Blanket (2026)

How heavy should a weighted blanket be? The 10% rule, a weight chart by body size, and five blankets from $45 to $269, prices checked Nov 28, 2026.

Share
9 min read
By GetSome Rest editorial team · Independent sleep product guides Published Nov 28, 2026 · Last updated Nov 28, 2026

A weighted blanket that is too light does nothing, and one that is too heavy feels like being pinned to the bed. The right weight is about 10% of your body weight — a 150 lb adult does best with a 15 lb blanket, a 200 lb adult with a 20 lb one. That 10% figure is a starting point, not a hard rule: body shape, heat tolerance and whether you share the bed all move the answer by a size. This guide gives you the chart, the five blankets worth buying at each weight, and the safety lines that matter. Prices checked November 28, 2026.

About 10% of your body weight: 100–130 lb → 10–12 lb blanket; 130–170 lb → 15 lb; 170–220 lb → 20 lb; 220 lb and up → 25 lb. If you are between sizes or new to weighted blankets, go lighter. Adults only — never for children under 3.

The 10% Rule and the Weight Chart

Nearly every brand uses the same starting rule: choose a blanket that weighs about 10% of your body weight. A 150 lb adult lands on a 15 lb blanket, a 200 lb adult on a 20 lb one. The 10% figure is a convention rather than a research finding — it comes from the deep-pressure therapy tradition, not from a clinical trial — but it is the rule every brand's sizing guide is built around, and it works as a starting point because it scales the pressure to the body underneath it.

Here is the chart most brands agree on:

1. Between sizes? Go lighter

If you are between two weights, or new to weighted blankets, choose the lighter one. A blanket that is slightly too light still feels calming; a blanket that is too heavy feels restrictive, makes it hard to turn over, and traps heat — and it is the blanket people stop using. You can always size up later.

2. Size matters as much as weight

A weighted blanket should cover your body, not your bed. Most are made for one person — a 15 lb blanket is typically 48 by 72 inches, which covers the top of a queen bed but does not reach across it. Buying a king-size blanket to cover the whole bed spreads the same weight over more area, which dilutes the pressure. If you share a bed, two individual blankets work better than one big one.

3. Fill type changes how the weight feels

Glass beads are the most common fill: small, dense, and sewn into pockets so the weight stays even. Knit blankets like the Bearaby use no fill at all — the weight comes from thick hand-knit cotton, and the open loops let air through, so they sleep cooler. Bead blankets hug the body edge to edge; knit blankets breathe better but cover less evenly. Hot sleepers should lean knit; everyone else should lean beads.

4. The safety lines

Weighted blankets are for adults. Never use one on a child under 3 years old, and a child should never use a blanket heavier than about 10% of their body weight — and only with a pediatrician's guidance. Adults with breathing difficulties, sleep apnea, or claustrophobia should also check with a clinician first, and anyone who feels pinned down or anxious under a blanket should size down or stop using it. The Blankets in Detail

YnM Weighted Blanket — about $45 for 15 lb queen

YnM is the best-selling weighted blanket on Amazon for good reason: it gets the details right at a price that makes trying a weighted blanket a low-risk experiment. The seven-layer design sandwiches glass beads between fabric layers, with small 2-inch pockets that keep the beads from bunching into one corner, and ties to hold an optional duvet cover. The weight range is the widest here — 5 to 30 lb — and YnM's own sizing guide is 10% of your body weight plus a pound. At about $45 for a 15 lb queen it costs a quarter of the premium picks. The compromise is the cover: it is a simple cotton shell, and the blanket is heavier to wash than the premium options. For a first weighted blanket, it is the obvious starting point.

Gravity Blanket — $189 to $199

The Gravity Blanket popularized the entire weighted-blanket category, and it still sets the standard for build quality. At $189–$199 it costs more than twice the YnM, and the premium buys fine-grade glass beads with less shifting over time, a removable and fully machine-washable micro-plush cover, and a more polished feel that reviewers consistently note side by side with budget options. It comes in 15, 20 and 25 lb — the three sizes that cover nearly every adult — If you already know you like weighted blankets and want the one you will keep for years, this is the pick.

Bearaby Cotton Napper — $249 to $269

The Bearaby Cotton Napper solves the heat problem differently: instead of glass beads, its weight comes entirely from the density of a thick, hand-knit organic cotton weave, with large open gaps built into the pattern that let air circulate. It is the most breathable blanket here and the best-looking one, and the 15 lb size measures 40 by 72 inches — it covers one person but does not reach across a queen bed. At $249–$269 it is the most expensive pick, and the open-knit structure means it does not hug the body edge to edge the way a bead-filled blanket does, so people who want maximum enclosed pressure may prefer YnM or Gravity instead. For hot sleepers, it is the only pick that solves the problem at the design level rather than with a cooling cover.

Baloo Weighted Blanket — about $200 to $220

Baloo is the pick for hot sleepers who still want beads. It is cotton inside and out, with glass microbeads and a fully machine-washable construction — the easiest blanket here to keep clean. It comes in 12 to 25 lb and sleeps cooler than the YnM or Gravity thanks to the all-cotton shell. The trade-off is the price: about $200–$220 puts it in premium territory without the Gravity's removable cover or the Bearaby's knit breathability. It is the best compromise if you want beads, cotton, and a washing machine in one purchase.

Luxome Weighted Blanket — $195 for 15 lb

Luxome sits between the budget and premium picks: $195 for a 15 lb blanket, with 1 mm glass beads — finer than most — in a removable cover. It comes in 15 to 25 lb and measures 54 by 72 inches for the 15 lb size, wider than the Gravity's 48 inches, so it covers more of the bed. The finer beads distribute more evenly and feel less lumpy than the YnM's, and the removable cover makes washing simple. It is the pick if you want a premium feel without paying Bearaby money, and the extra width makes it the best of the five for people who move around at night.

Frequently asked questions

What weight weighted blanket should I get?

Start at about 10% of your body weight, rounded to the nearest available size: 100 to 130 lb → 10 to 12 lb; 130 to 170 lb → 15 lb; 170 to 220 lb → 20 lb; 220 lb and up → 25 lb. If you are between sizes or new to weighted blankets, go lighter — you can always size up later, and a blanket that is too heavy is the one people stop using.

Can a weighted blanket be too heavy?

Yes. A blanket above roughly 10% of your body weight can feel restrictive instead of calming, make it hard to turn over, and trap heat. Most brands top out at 25 lb for adults for this reason. If you feel pinned down, hot, or anxious under the blanket, it is too heavy — size down, or try a knit blanket like the Bearaby, which breathes better than a bead-filled one.

Are weighted blankets safe for kids?

Weighted blankets are made for adults. They should never be used for children under 3 years old, and a child should never use a blanket heavier than about 10% of their body weight — and only with guidance from a pediatrician, especially for children with breathing issues or developmental conditions. This guide covers adult sizes only.

Can two people share one weighted blanket?

It rarely works well. A shared blanket needs to cover two bodies, so the weight is spread across both people and neither gets the full 10% pressure — and most blankets are sized for one person anyway. Couples usually do better with two individual blankets, or a king-size blanket in the heavier 25 lb range if both partners are similar in size.

How do you wash a weighted blanket?

It depends on the fill. The Gravity Blanket has a removable machine-washable cover, and the Baloo is machine washable inside and out. Bead-filled blankets without a removable cover usually need a gentle cycle and low-heat drying, and the heavy weight strains a home washer — use a large-capacity machine or a laundromat. Knit blankets like the Bearaby are hand-wash or gentle cycle only, and take a long time to dry flat.

  • Start at 10% of your body weight: 130–170 lb → 15 lb; 170–220 lb → 20 lb; 220 lb and up → 25 lb.
  • Between sizes, go lighter: a blanket that is slightly too light still feels calming; one that is too heavy is the one people stop using.
  • Our winner is the YnM at about $45: the best-selling weighted blanket on Amazon, with the widest weight range and the lowest-risk price for a first try.
  • Hot sleepers should go knit: the Bearaby Cotton Napper at $249–$269 breathes through open loops instead of trapping heat like bead blankets.
  • Size for one person, not the bed: a blanket that covers the whole bed spreads the weight too thin — couples do better with two individual blankets.
  • Adults only: never use a weighted blanket on a child under 3, and keep any child's blanket at or under 10% of their body weight with a pediatrician's guidance.
